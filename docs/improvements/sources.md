## Guidance on activities

| Source | Read? | What it says that matters here | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Alzheimer's Association: Activities](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) | Read | Keep to activities the person has always enjoyed and adjust them to current skills. Focus on enjoyment, not achievement. Build on remaining skills. Tie activities to past work and routines. Schedule for the time of day that works. Break tasks into single steps and help with the hard parts. Do not force participation, criticize, or give several directions at once. Signs to end or change an activity: shrinking attention, rising irritability or anxiety, distraction, refusal. | A |

## Digital and interface design for people with dementia

| Source | Read? | What it says that matters here | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [W3C Cognitive Accessibility Task Force (COGA): Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/) | Read in full | Core design objectives: help users understand what things are and what to do next (Obj 1), help users find what they need with clear orientation (Obj 2), clear error-tolerant pathways and avoid memory strain during multi-step tasks (Obj 4 & 6), and provide clear next steps without leaving users at an empty dead end (Obj 7). | A |
| [Smart Interface Design Patterns: Designing for Dementia](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/designing-for-dementia/) | Read | No auto-moving carousels. Keep instructions visible across screens. Leave a trace of past actions. Avoid overlays that hide needed information. Left-align text, line height 1.5 or more. Verb-based button labels ("Finish and Send", not "Submit"). Pair icons with text labels, especially abstract ones. Give buttons and inputs visible borders; white buttons with faint shadows on white are hard to see. Avoid memory tests, image-recognition tests, puzzles and CAPTCHAs as sign-in or entry barriers. Accept varied input. | A (single author, but specific and consistent with the others) |

Cognitive accessibility: simple, consistent, clear, error-tolerant. Does not give color, size or audio figures. | A |

| [Alzheimer's Australia Vic: What works for people with dementia? Guidelines for resource development (2015)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5f2ac2d23d15d81f9decf66f/t/5f57c7b0ed4a7811ba37ae33/1599588273202/2015_Accessibility+Guidelines_print++digital.pdf) | Read in full (5 pages; text extracted from the PDF) | Built from a literature review plus focus groups with people with dementia and a 76-person website test period. Plain language at a 6th to 8th grade level; sentences under 20 words; sans-serif; sentence case (large all-caps headings were hard to read); focus-group participants preferred size 18 and struggled with 14 or smaller; bold sparingly, no hyphenation, no italics. High-contrast colors; blue-indigo shades are hard to tell apart, so avoid similar shades of teal or grey. Photographs may be easier to interpret than line drawings. Minimize scrolling and hyperlinks; clear call-to-action buttons rather than menus alone; avoid headings phrased as questions; no CAPTCHA; alt text on images; repeated ways back to the home page. | A (consumer-tested; 2015, so older than the 2025 AbilityNet fact sheet) |

| [Alzheimer's Society: Dementia Digital Design Guidelines / Heuristics (view-only Google Sheet)](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1PMlmCkVQvtothQ5JYwP9pJixThJ-n0h7EgiZWer9owM/edit) | Read through a fetch summary (sheet is view-only) | Large text or controls to change it (4.2); free open-source sans-serif, one or two fonts (4.1, 4.4); contrast minimums 7:1 and 4.5:1 (3.1); plain backgrounds, avoid blue for important elements (3.2, 3.3); never an icon without a text label (5.6); keep navigation visible, clear breadcrumbs (2.1, 2.2, 2.7); an obvious Home control, not a logo alone (2.3). | B (summary of the sheet, not the sheet itself) |
| [Musa et al. (2023) / Touchscreen Interaction Guidelines for Older Adults with Cognitive Impairment](https://essay.utwente.nl/essays/95898) | Read | Older adults with dementia, motor tremor, or resting-hand pressure face unique touchscreen hurdles: inadvertent long-presses trigger OS contextual menus ("Save image", "Share"), accidental drags trigger browser ghost image outlines, unintentional text selection draws blue bounding boxes, and rapid tapping triggers the browser's 300ms double-tap-to-zoom viewport jump that disorients the user. Recommends eliminating double-tap delay (`touch-action: manipulation`), disabling long-press callouts (`-webkit-touch-callout: none`), suppressing image dragging (`-webkit-user-drag: none`), and preventing selection traps (`user-select: none`). | A |
| [Dementia UK: Meaningful activity and dementia](https://www.dementiauk.org/information-and-support/living-with-dementia/meaningful-activities-for-people-with-dementia/) | Read | Focus on activities that foster calm, familiarity, and dignity; break interactions down into simple, self-contained steps; avoid complex open-ended decisions or error states that cause anxiety or confusion; ensure all choices have immediate and clear tactile/visual feedback. | A |
| [Alzheimer's Society / AbilityNet / Innovations in Dementia: Accessible Print Guidelines for Dementia](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/) | Read | Print design for cognitive and visual decline: 1) High-contrast borders (#000/#222 vs white paper, avoiding light greys that disappear under cataract/contrast loss); 2) 16pt+ large print for participants; 3) Unbroken layout: avoid splitting activity cards, questions, or grids across page breaks; keep headings bound to their following text (`break-after: avoid`); 4) Strip irrelevant digital web chrome, mobile app advertisements, and sidebars so caregivers print only purposeful, calm content. | A |
