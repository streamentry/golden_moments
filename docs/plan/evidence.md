# Evidence for activity design

Compiled 2026-10-31 from web search and opened abstracts or full texts.
Grades: **E** established (Cochrane, large or replicated RCTs), **P** probable
(smaller RCTs, consistent qualitative work), **W** weak (pilot, vendor or
snippet-only). "Snippet-only" means the source could not be opened and the
claim rests on a search excerpt. This appendix informs
[the plan](README.md); it does not validate any clinical outcome, and nothing
in it licenses a benefit claim in the app or the store.

## 1. Reminiscence, including digital and photo-based

- Cochrane 2018 (Woods et al., 22 RCTs, n=1,972): effects "inconsistent, often
  small". Quality of life: no overall effect (SMD 0.11), probable slight benefit
  in care homes (SMD 0.46, 3 studies). Cognition: tiny end-of-treatment gain of
  doubtful importance, not sustained. Communication: probable slight benefit at
  follow-up. Individual reminiscence: slight benefit on depression. No benefit to
  carer stress; possible small rise in carer anxiety when carers joined groups.
  **E**. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29493789/>
- Digital reminiscence meta-analysis (BMC Neurology 2026; 13 studies, 7 RCTs,
  n=718): no significant pooled effect on cognition, mood, quality of life or
  engagement; small reduction in behavioral symptoms from only 2 RCTs.
  Personalized, socially engaging formats looked most favorable. **P**.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13137691/>
- Online Life Story Book RCT (PLoS One 2021, n=42 dyads at home): no effect on
  neuropsychiatric symptoms; a transient drop in caregiver distress at 3 months.
  **P** (null). <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8443059/>
- Fotoscope photo-conversation pilot (JMIR Formative Research 2025, 20 nursing-home
  wards, 83 dyads): generic photos on a tablet, 30-minute sessions. Residents rated
  conversations more positively than control; staff said they got to know residents
  better; 86% of residents said it worked well on a tablet. 30 minutes was too long
  for later stages; login failures and staff time were the main barriers. **P**.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044310/>
- Credible to claim: enjoyment in the moment, conversation, mood during and shortly
  after a session, staff or family getting to know the person. Not credible:
  cognition, memory, disease course, lasting quality-of-life gains, caregiver
  burden. **E**.

## 2. Music and sound

- Cochrane 2025 (van der Steen et al., 30 RCTs, n=1,720): music probably slightly
  improves depressive symptoms, may improve overall behavioral problems, and
  **likely has no effect on agitation or aggression**. No long-term effects. **E**.
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40049590/>
- Music & Memory personalized playlists: a 2017 matched comparison of nursing homes
  found more antipsychotic discontinuation; the 2022 METRIcAL cluster RCT (54 homes,
  n=976) found no effect on agitation or psychotropic use. **E** that the controlled
  effect is null or small. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28483436/>,
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9256757/>
- Dutch focus groups (Kerkhof et al. 2017): carers found background music irritating
  and wanted it switchable off; a sound as a confirmation cue was welcomed. **P**.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6453092>

Implication: keep library music off by default (the current decision), no background
music in gentle activities, optional soft confirmation sounds, no music licensing.

## 3. Errorless learning, Montessori, failure-free design

- Errorless versus errorful learning in MCI and dementia: meta-analysis (Roberts,
  Jones and Clare 2012; 9 studies, n=110) summary effect 0.65, called "tentative".
  **P** (snippet-only).
- Montessori-based activities (Sheppard et al. 2016, 14 studies): strong evidence
  for eating difficulties, strong to weak for engagement and affect, weak for
  cognition. Engagement is the consistent outcome. **P**.
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26589532/>
- Qualitative work on casual tablet games reports failure, annoyance and insecurity
  as the negative experiences; design papers recommend no negative feedback on
  failure. **W to P** (snippet-only). <https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/75404/>
- Joddrell and Astell 2016: spoken prompts were often ignored or over-relied on,
  producing passive use; avatars were rejected as distracting; feedback should be
  immediate and tied to the user's own input. **P**.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5454556/>
- No controlled study compares timed or scored against untimed digital activities
  in dementia. The no-timer, no-score rule rests on qualitative and guideline
  evidence only.

## 4. Touchscreen usability and shared play

- Joddrell and Astell 2016 (45 studies): people with dementia can use touchscreens
  independently; independence tracked motivation more than cognitive level. Few
  steps, uncluttered, consistent icons with text, large elements, no drop-downs,
  no icon-only buttons; multitouch causes accidental gestures from a resting hand;
  no single right screen size. **P**.
- Joddrell and Astell 2019 (n=30): dementia accessibility settings added to a
  mainstream Solitaire improved usability and the ability to start play; enjoyment
  unchanged. **P**. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018201/>
- AcTo Dementia (Sheffield) app-selection criteria: interaction, feedback,
  aesthetics, customization, obstacles such as ads and pop-ups, age-appropriateness.
  Enjoyment in group tablet sessions was real but "momentary and did not carry over
  between sessions". **P**. <https://www.actodementia.com/evidence->
- Kerkhof et al. 2017 user requirements (mild dementia and carers, 8 focus groups):
  large spaced buttons; a clear Home control; controls in the same place on every
  screen; minimal scrolling and typing; strong contrast; no animation or
  auto-updating content; no childish design; both orientations; tolerate accidental
  taps, hard presses and double taps; undo; no passwords; **everything in the
  user's language, a mixed-language interface confused people**; music off. **P**.
- Smith, Mountain and Hawkins 2020 (12 people with moderate dementia in day care):
  tablets enabled enjoyable group participation only with effective support. **P**.
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30269531/>
- I-CARE tablet system (9 dyads at home): feasible, participants valued a shared
  activity, independent use needed sustained support. **W**.
  <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9518490/>
- Tovertafel: short-term apathy reduction in a 25-resident uncontrolled study, not
  persisting; no adequately powered RCT. **W**.
  <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11638534/>
- Menorah Park Engagement Scale: observational, 5-minute windows, used as a trial
  outcome; Korean validation alpha 0.90. **P** (original 2000 paper not opened).
- Not found: evidence-based tap-target sizes, icon-versus-word or swipe-versus-tap
  trials specific to dementia.

## 5. Caregiver needs and app abandonment

- AARP and NAC, Caregiving in the US 2025: 63 million caregivers; about 40% say
  respite would help; 1 in 5 report poor health from caregiving. **E**.
  <https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2025-07-24-new-report-reveals-crisis-point-for-americas-63-million-family-caregivers>
- Alzheimer's Association Facts and Figures 2026: 12.7 million unpaid dementia
  caregivers; 59% report high or very high emotional stress; about 30% are aged
  65 or over. **E**. <https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.71345>
- JMIR mHealth 2025 meta-synthesis (12 qualitative studies, 232 caregivers):
  caregivers value skill-building, burden relief, emotional help and social
  connection; barriers are design flaws, low tech literacy, time, privacy and
  device issues; some stopped because apps "failed to deliver sufficient added
  value". **P**. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40532203/>
- JMIR Aging 2018: caregiver apps are largely static text with little evidence.
  JMIR 2023 review of 50 researched apps flags usability, privacy and security
  concerns. **P**. <https://doi.org/10.2196/12274>, <https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188>

## 6. Sundowning and agitation

- Livingston et al. 2014 (33 RCTs): person-centered care, communication training and
  care mapping reduce agitation; activities and music by protocol reduce overall
  agitation (effect sizes 0.5 to 0.6); sensory interventions help only while in
  place; aromatherapy and light therapy did not show efficacy. **E**.
  <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25452601/>
- Cochrane light therapy (Forbes 2014): no effect on cognition, sleep, agitation or
  psychiatric symptoms. **E**.
- Multisensory stimulation meta-analysis (2025, 16 RCTs, n=974): large effects on
  agitation and apathy with wide intervals and high heterogeneity. **P**.
- Honest wording for the app: "a calm, familiar activity some families use during
  restless times". "Reduces sundowning" or "reduces agitation" is not supportable;
  no trial tests a photo app for either. **E** (absence of evidence).

## 7. Care homes and day centers

- England adult social care provider technology survey 2025 (n=1,085): barriers are
  set-up cost 73%, ongoing license cost 70%, staff training and turnover 52%,
  connectivity 40%, staff digital skills 39%; 25% use health or well-being apps. **E**.
  <https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/technology-use-in-adult-social-care-2025-survey-results/findings-from-the-2025-adult-social-care-provider-technology-survey>
- UK activity providers: funding, time, training and retention gaps; many homes have
  no ring-fenced activity budget. **P** (snippet).
- Ontario long-term-care tablet study (PLoS One 2025, 7 homes): adoption hinged on
  workflow integration, tech support and staff motivation. **P**.
- Pricing: Tovertafel and LifeLoop/iN2L are quote-only; nothing public. **W**.
- Not found: any study of what activity coordinators look for when choosing apps.

## 8. Accessibility standards

- W3C COGA "Making Content Usable" (Note, 2021) addresses age-related memory loss
  and early dementia: clear purpose, clear words, no timeouts or data loss, easy
  undo, limited interruptions, no memory-based login, user control over moving
  content, consistency. **E**. <https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/>
- WCAG 2.2 AA items with cognitive relevance: 2.5.8 target size, 3.2.6 consistent
  help, 3.3.7 redundant entry, 3.3.8 accessible authentication. AA conformance does
  not cover cognitive accessibility. **E**.
- Apple Accessibility Nutrition Labels (announced May 2025, shown on iOS 26):
  voluntary now, to become mandatory for new apps and updates with no date given.
  A label counts only if all common tasks complete with that feature. **E**.
  <https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/manage-app-accessibility/overview-of-accessibility-nutrition-labels>
- EU Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882, applies from 28 June 2025): apps are in
  scope as part of listed services such as e-commerce; microenterprises are exempt;
  a recreational app with no e-commerce is likely out of scope, in-app purchase may
  touch it. **P** (directive text could not be opened).

## 9. Reading aloud

- Older adults understand and remember synthetic speech less well than natural
  speech, even slowed; no study of text-to-speech comprehension in dementia was
  found. **P** for older adults, **W** for dementia (snippet-only).
- Spoken prompts were often ignored or produced passive reliance (Joddrell and
  Astell 2016). **P**.
- Platform speech: iOS `AVSpeechSynthesizer` runs on device with default voices per
  language; Android depends on the installed engine and flags network voices and
  missing language data. Verify against current documentation before claiming
  offline speech in any language. **P** (API knowledge, pages did not render).

## 10. Consent, privacy and legacy

- Alzheimer's Society factsheet 437 "Using technology": no decision without the
  person's consent; where capacity is lacking, decide on their behalf under the
  Mental Capacity Act; technology must not replace face-to-face contact. **E**.
  <https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/media/16636>
- Alzheimer Europe, January 2026, guidelines for the ethical use of technology,
  co-written with people with dementia and carers: exists; content not extracted. **P**.
  <https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/resources/publications/discussion-paper-and-guidelines-ethical-use-technology-and-people-dementia>
- Research consent (Thorogood et al. 2018): presume capacity unless established
  otherwise. **E**.
- No dementia-specific digital-legacy research was found. **W**.

## 11. Bilingualism and language reversion

- Mendez 2019 (Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, review): as executive control
  declines, bilingual people with Alzheimer's have increasing difficulty staying
  in the second language; second-language skills decline more steeply; reversion
  toward the first language is reported regardless of education or baseline
  fluency. **P** (review, not meta-analysis). <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31381516/>
- Care implications (Swedish video studies; Welsh scoping work): monolingual staff
  had severe problems communicating with residents who had reverted to their first
  language; language-matched staff relationships were positive; family members act
  as go-betweens. **P** (small qualitative studies, snippet-only).
  <https://bmcgeriatr.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12877-016-0200-y>
- Research is skewed to English-Spanish, early-stage, balanced bilinguals. **P**.

Implication: the participant's activity language should be settable independently
of the helper's; prompts and captions in the participant's first language, with a
second caption line so a mismatched helper can follow. Never promise that this
restores language.

## Design implications for Golden Moments

1. Market enjoyment, conversation and connection only; never cognition, memory,
   agitation, sundowning or caregiver-burden benefits.
2. Caregiver-selected photos with open prompts match the formats that fared best.
   Design for 5 to 15-minute sessions with "Done for now" always visible.
3. No timers, scores, streaks or wrong-answer feedback; immediate neutral feedback
   tied to the user's own tap; visible-picture matching is consistent with the
   errorless-learning literature.
4. Large, spaced targets; tolerate double taps, hard presses and resting-hand
   touches; undo; same control positions on every screen; no drop-downs, no
   icon-only buttons, no animation or auto-advancing content, no passwords.
5. No background music; soft optional confirmation sounds; read-aloud optional,
   off by default and lower priority than first assumed.
6. Caregiver co-use is the realistic mode for moderate dementia; keep helper setup
   short, because staff time and setup friction killed uptake in Fotoscope and the
   Dutch focus groups.
7. Separate participant and helper languages; bilingual captions; no mixed-language
   essential controls.
8. A short in-app consent note: choose photos the person is happy to see; local-only
   storage fits Alzheimer's Society and Alzheimer Europe guidance.
9. Prepare Accessibility Nutrition Label evidence now.
10. Measure usability by observed engagement and in-the-moment enjoyment, never by
    pre and post cognition.
