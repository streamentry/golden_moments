"I want to go home." For many families this is one of the hardest things to hear, especially when the person you care for is sitting in their own living room. It is common, it can happen at any stage of dementia, and there are calm, practical ways to respond. This page covers what the words often mean, what to say, activities that can help in the moment, and how to keep them safe.

## Hafa na i taotao ni guaha dementia ha sodda' para u hanao gi guma'?

Usually, "home" is a feeling more than a place. The Alzheimer's Society describes it as a sense of comfort and security, or a home from the past, rather than the house itself. Dementia can make familiar rooms look strange, and a person may feel they are living in an earlier time of life, when home was a childhood house or the place where they raised their children.

The words can also be a sign that something else is wrong right now: they are hungry, thirsty, need the toilet, are in pain, too hot or cold, tired, lonely, frightened, or overwhelmed by noise. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-changes-behavior-and-communication/coping-agitation-aggression-and-sundowning) lists pain, noise, loneliness, changes in routine and medicines among the causes of agitation.

## Hafa para u sangane anai ha sodda' para u hanao gi guma'?

Respond to the feeling, not the words. The [Family Caregiver Alliance](https://www.caregiver.org/resource/caregivers-guide-understanding-dementia-behaviors/) advises against trying to convince the person they are wrong: their feelings are real, so answer with comfort and reassurance first, then gently redirect.

Phrases that often help:

- **Sangane i siente.** "Ha sinenti na un miku i guma'."
- **Na'seguru.** "Seguru hao giya, yan hu egga' hao." The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/stages-behaviors/anxiety-agitation) suggests the simple words "You're safe here."
- **Kombida i inadahi.** "Sangane yu' put i guma'-mu. Hafa i kusina-mu?" Asking about the distant past is usually more comfortable than questions about today.
- **Ofrese unu para u na'la'la' yan iya.** "Ininum ta un kapin te gi antes," or "Siña un ayudu yu' yan este na tuwåya?"

A warm tone and a hand on the arm, if they like touch, can say more than the words.

## Hafa para u evita?

- **Inagång pat explika.** "Esta hao gi guma' påpa'" or "Este i guma'-mu para 40 años" asks them to accept something dementia makes hard to accept, and it can feel like being told they are wrong.
- **Sodda' i inadahi.** "Ti un inadahi?" or "Hafa i direksion?" adds pressure.
- **Blok i petta gi anti.** If they head out, stay calm and walk with them. A short walk together often settles the urge.
- **Apurå.** Give them time to talk about home before you change the subject.

## Na'an siña ayudu gi halom i tiempo

After you have acknowledged the feeling, a calm, familiar activity can shift attention without dismissing it.

1. **Atan i litratu i guma' yan i familia.** Turn the pages and make comments ("Look at that garden") rather than asking questions. On a phone or tablet, [Photo pairs](/games/photo-pairs/) turns your own family photos into a gentle matching game, and the photos stay on your device.
2. **Sangane put i guma'.** [Conversation cards](/games/conversation-cards/) offer open prompts about houses, kitchens, neighbors and holidays, with no right answers.
3. **Toka i musika ni ha guaiya.** The National Institute on Aging lists soothing music among the ways to calm agitation. See our [music activities](/activities/music/).
4. **Na'na' na ni'ok pat ininum.** Hunger and thirst can look like restlessness. A cup of tea or a favorite treat is also a small ritual of being at home.
5. **Binu pat seta gi halom i tåno'.** Movement and daylight help, especially earlier in the day.
6. **Na'fanmåså' un tårea ni mas tåtaotao.** Folding laundry, sorting cutlery or watering plants can bring back the feeling of running a home.
7. **Na'fanmåså' todo.** Turn off the TV, cut down noise and glare, and try a shared [calm breathing](/games/calm-breathing/) pause.

If one idea does not land, try another later. Sitting quietly together is a good outcome too.

The Alzheimer's Society suggests keeping a short log of when the asking happens, such as before meals, after visitors, or at dusk. Patterns make it easier to plan ahead.

## OK na para u sangane un white lie?

This is a real debate, and kind families land in different places. The Alzheimer's Society says there is no single right answer: untruths can make some people more suspicious, so decide what is in the person's best interests and be consistent. At the Penn Memory Center, the co-director's view is that truth is the default and a gentle untruth is acceptable only when the person cannot process a particular truth, and that you should never mock their reality.

Many caregivers find a middle path works: honest comfort and redirection first ("We'll stay here tonight, and I'm right here with you"), without arguing about where home is.

## Hafa si ha na'fanlå'la' gi kada påpa' pat puengi?

Restlessness that starts or gets worse in the late afternoon is often called sundowning. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-changes-behavior-and-communication/coping-agitation-aggression-and-sundowning) suggests a steady schedule, daily sunlight and activity, fewer late naps, and no caffeine or alcohol late in the day. Turning lights on before dusk can also help. Our guide to [calm late-afternoon activities](/caregiving/late-day-restlessness/) has more.

## Na'seguru na u mafnas si ha intenta u hanao

Asking to go home can lead to wandering. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/stages-behaviors/wandering) estimates that six in ten people living with dementia will wander at least once.

**Planu gi antes:**

- An ID or medical bracelet with their name and your phone number, and labels in their clothes. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-changes-behavior-and-communication/coping-alzheimers-behaviors-wandering-and) mentions the MedicAlert and Alzheimer's Association Safe and Found program, which may cost money.
- A door chime or alarm, and locks placed high or low. Never lock the person in alone.
- A recent close-up photo, and a list of places they might go, such as a former home or workplace.
- Tell trusted neighbors, and give them your number.

**Si ha mafnas:** check nearby safe places while contacting local police or emergency services promptly. Do not wait a fixed number of minutes before reporting. A 2021 [U.S. Department of Justice journal article on missing-person investigations](https://www.justice.gov/usdoj-media/usao/media/1134031/dl?inline=) says law-enforcement agencies should not require a set waiting period; it also lists dementia-like symptoms as a high-risk concern. Local reporting procedures vary. Tell responders the person has dementia, share the last known location and a recent photo, and follow their directions. If another helper is available, ask them to stay by the phone. Do not search unsafe places alone.

## Anai para u ågang un doktor?

Call their doctor promptly if the asking or confusion starts suddenly, or gets much worse over hours or days. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-changes-behavior-and-communication/alzheimers-caregiving-managing-personality-and) says sudden or rapidly changing behavior, especially after an infection or a medicine change, should be checked right away. Sudden confusion can be delirium, which the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/) says needs urgent help; urinary tract infections are a common cause in older people.

It also helps to mention a steady pattern at the next appointment, since pain, low mood, sleep problems or medicine side effects can all play a part.
