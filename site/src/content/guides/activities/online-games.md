Many families search for free online games for dementia because they want something to do together that does not need a store, a login or a credit card. The best options are simple, picture-based, and easy to stop. They should feel like a shared moment, not a test. This guide explains what to look for, how to set up a game, and what these games can and cannot do.

## What makes an online game suitable for dementia?

A game suits most people living with dementia when it asks for recognition rather than recall. Matching two identical pictures, tapping the picture that matches a shadow, or dropping an item into the right basket all rely on what the eyes can see right now. Games that depend on remembering a rule, a word, a number or a previous move tend to cause frustration.

The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) recommends activities that build on what a person still enjoys and can do, and that let them succeed without help. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-caregiving/adapting-activities-people-alzheimers-disease) suggests simplifying an activity rather than giving up on it when abilities change.

## Golden Moments games you can open in a browser

Each game below opens from the website with no download. Most play on a tablet or computer; some can also be printed.

- **[Picture pairs](/games/picture-pairs/).** Large matching pairs with the pictures showing, and no timer or score. Good for a first game.
- **[Find the same](/games/find-the-same/).** One picture at the top, and the same one among two to four choices below.
- **[Shadow match](/games/shadow-match/).** A soft shadow appears at the top; tap the picture that makes it.
- **[Sort together](/games/sort-together/).** Tap a picture, then the basket it belongs in, such as kitchen or garden.
- **[Picture puzzle](/games/picture-puzzle/).** A picture is cut into pieces and the whole image stays visible as a guide.
- **[Finish the saying](/games/finish-the-saying/).** Familiar sayings with the last word missing; tap a choice or show the ending.
- **[Conversation cards](/games/conversation-cards/).** Picture cards with open prompts for talking, not quizzing. Also printable.
- **[Photo pairs](/games/photo-pairs/).** Matching pairs made from photos you choose on your device. The photos stay in the browser on that device.
- **[Calm breathing](/games/calm-breathing/).** A slow, quiet pause to breathe together. You start it and you stop it.

Word search, coloring and picture bingo are also available as printable activities. See [free printable dementia activities](/activities/printable/) for more.

## How to set up a game session

1. **Choose a quiet time** when the person is rested. Late afternoon is often harder for some people, so check your own pattern first.
2. **Use a larger screen.** A tablet on a stand, or a computer with the browser zoomed in, is easier to see and touch.
3. **Close other tabs and turn off notifications.** Sudden pop-ups are startling and can end a good session.
4. **Sit beside the person, not across from them.** Shared viewing feels more like company and less like a test.
5. **Start with one game for five to ten minutes.** Let the person touch the screen or point while you take turns.
6. **End on a good note.** Finish a round, then talk about one picture or suggest a cup of tea.

## Privacy and safety on shared devices

- **Photos stay on the device.** Photo pairs use photos you choose, and they stay in the browser on that device. Check who else can open the computer or tablet.
- **Avoid external links and ads.** Use the games directly from the site, and do not click banners or prompts that ask for personal details.
- **Keep payments and accounts separate.** Do not leave the person alone on a screen that asks for a password or a card number.
- **Watch for wandering.** Keep a tablet out of reach if the person may leave the house with it.

## What these games can and cannot do

Online games give people with dementia a chance to enjoy a moment, to look at pictures together, and to take part in something without pressure. They do not treat dementia, slow it, or prevent it. A widely reported example is the [Federal Trade Commission's 2016 action against Lumosity](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program) over brain-training claims that could not be backed up. Be wary of any game, app or website that promises better memory, scores your brain or says it can reverse decline.

Use these games as one part of a day that also includes music, walks, conversation and rest. For reading on how to make games relaxed rather than tense, see [how to play games with someone who has dementia](/caregiving/how-to-play-together/) and [memory games that do not test](/activities/memory-games/).

## When to stop

Stop when the person looks tired, restless, frustrated or uneasy. Stop when they lose interest. Stopping early is a good outcome. You can always come back another day, or switch to conversation or a walk instead.
