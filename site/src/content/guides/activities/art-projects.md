Art projects are a rewarding way to spend time with someone who has dementia. They engage the hands, the eyes and the senses, and they give you something to talk about. This page offers specific projects with simple steps, plus guidance on choosing supplies safely. For the principles behind adult art, such as avoiding childish materials, see [arts and crafts for adults with dementia](/activities/arts-and-crafts/).

## How to start any art project

1. **Set up before you begin.** Lay out one or two materials, a protective sheet and a damp cloth.
2. **Show the first step.** Dip the brush, tear the first piece, or squeeze a small lump of clay, and invite them to do the same.
3. **Work side by side.** Keep your own project nearby so the activity feels shared, not supervised.
4. **Talk about what you see.** Describe colors, textures and shapes rather than judging the work.
5. **Stop before the energy drops.** Ten to twenty minutes is often a good length.

The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/art-music) notes that art is a way to express feeling and be creative without needing artistic skill. The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-caregiving/adapting-activities-people-alzheimers-disease) advises breaking activities into small, manageable steps.

## Watercolor wash

Use heavy watercolor paper, a large soft brush and a few colors in shallow cups. Wet the paper with clean water, then let them drop color onto it. Tilt the paper gently to let the colors run. The result is a soft, abstract picture that needs no plan.

- **Early stage:** Let them choose colors and paint a landscape.
- **Middle stage:** Offer two or three colors and do the wetting for them.
- **Later stage:** Hold the paper while they dab with a sponge.

## Magazine collage

Gather magazine pages with bright photos of flowers, food, animals, cars or travel. Tear the pictures in large pieces, which is easier than cutting, and glue them onto a sheet of card. Collage works well as a conversation starter, because each picture can lead to a memory. It suits a person who likes sorting and choosing.

## Soft clay

Air-dry clay or soft modeling clay can be pinched, rolled and squeezed. Make a simple bowl or a flat tile. Clay is strongly tactile, so it can be calming for people who like to handle things. Keep pieces large, and avoid tools with sharp points.

## Chalk pastels on dark paper

Dark paper makes pastel colors glow. Use large chunky pastels and let the person make broad strokes or blend with a finger or a tissue. Pastels are dusty, so set up on a tray and wipe hands afterward. Choose soft pastels that are labeled non-toxic.

## Leaf printing

Collect large leaves from the garden, brush washable paint onto the veins, and press the leaf onto paper. Peel it off carefully and admire the print. This project links well to [outdoor and gardening activities](/activities/outdoor-and-gardening/), and it can start with a walk to collect leaves.

## Fabric and flower arranging

Sort textured fabric swatches by feel, or arrange a few fresh flowers in a vase. These are sensory projects with very little pressure. They also suit people in the later stages. For more ideas, see [sensory activities for people with dementia](/activities/sensory/).

## Choosing art supplies

Supplies matter more for safety and comfort than for quality. When you choose, consider:

- **Non-toxic and washable:** Look for labels that say non-toxic and check that paints are washable.
- **Large grips:** Jumbo crayons, thick brushes and chunky pastels are easier to hold.
- **No small parts:** Avoid beads, buttons, sequins and loose glitter that could be swallowed.
- **No sharp tools:** Use safety scissors or tear paper by hand.
- **Allergies:** Check for latex, and for any known skin sensitivities.
- **Contrast:** Bold colors on plain white or on dark paper are easier to see.
- **A short list:** Two or three materials are enough. Too many choices can overwhelm.
- **Easy cleanup:** Use a tray, a plastic sheet and a damp cloth, so mess does not become a reason to stop.

## Adapting by stage

In the early stage, most people can plan and complete a project with a little help. In the middle stage, offer fewer choices and more guidance. In the late stage, focus on sensory processes like squeezing clay or dabbing paint with a sponge, and enjoy the result rather than the finish.

For more stage-specific ideas, see [early-stage](/activities/early-stage/), [middle-stage](/activities/middle-stage/) and [late-stage activities](/activities/late-stage/). If the person refuses or becomes frustrated, our guide on [when someone says no](/caregiving/when-they-say-no/) explains how to step back.

## Pair with a game

After a painting session, try [coloring](/games/coloring/) on a tablet for a quieter follow-up, or a [picture puzzle](/games/picture-puzzle/) for a finishing activity. Both keep hands busy without asking for a fine skill.
