Coloring is one of the easiest creative activities to offer someone living with Alzheimer's or another form of dementia. It needs no reading, no memory and no right answer. Hands move, colors come out, and there is a picture to talk about. This guide covers how to choose pages, what to set out, and how to coloring together without making it feel childish or like a test.

## Why coloring suits many people with dementia

Coloring gives the hands something to do and the eyes something to follow. It can be done sitting at a table or in a chair by a window, and it can be picked up and put down easily. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/art-music) describes art as a way to express feelings and enjoy the present, and notes that it does not require artistic skill.

The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-caregiving/adapting-activities-people-alzheimers-disease) advises choosing activities that match what a person can still do, and adjusting them as abilities change. Coloring is easy to adjust: a bigger picture, thicker crayons, fewer colors, or a tap-to-color screen.

## Choose adult designs

Adults often dislike childish pictures. Look for:

- **Nature scenes:** a garden with big flowers, a lake with a boat, birds on a branch.
- **Familiar objects:** a teapot, a bicycle, a piece of fruit, a hat.
- **Large, simple patterns:** mandalas and bold geometric shapes with a few large sections.
- **Art prints or landscapes** that the person may recognize from a favorite place or painter.

Avoid pages with tiny, crowded areas, which are frustrating to fill. Printable adult pages are a good option, and our [free printable dementia activities](/activities/printable/) include large-print pages.

## What to set out

- **Thick, easy-to-hold tools.** Jumbo crayons, chunky markers, or colored pencils with a wide grip are easier than thin pens.
- **A small palette.** Three or four colors are enough. Too many choices can be overwhelming.
- **Sturdy paper or card.** Thin paper tears and slides. Tape the corners down or use a clipboard.
- **A clear table.** Remove clutter and set one page at a time.
- **Something to dip or blend,** like a cloth or a sponge, for a softer effect.

## Coloring on a tablet

The [coloring game](/games/coloring/) on Golden Moments works by tapping a color and then tapping a part of the picture. It needs no fine hand control, so it can suit people whose grip has weakened. Sit beside the person so you can share the screen, and let them choose the colors. Turn off notifications so the picture stays the focus.

## How to color together

1. **Begin the first stroke together.** Hold the crayon with them, or put your hand over theirs for a few strokes, then let go.
2. **Talk about colors and places.** "That's a lovely blue. Where have you seen water that color?" invites conversation without quizzing.
3. **Praise the choices, not the neatness.** "You chose such warm colors" feels better than "You stayed in the lines."
4. **Let them lead.** If they want to scribble over the whole page, let them.
5. **Stop when they stop.** Put the crayons down, and suggest a cup of tea or a walk.

## Adapting as dementia changes

In the early stage, people often enjoy detailed pages and can choose their own colors and designs. In the middle stage, larger pictures and fewer choices help. In later stages, try a single big shape, a soft-colored sheet, or simply holding a crayon while you hum a tune. Some people enjoy looking at a finished picture more than making one.

For more on creative work at each stage, see [arts and crafts for adults with dementia](/activities/arts-and-crafts/) and [art projects for people with dementia](/activities/art-projects/).

## When to stop

Stop if the person becomes frustrated, pushes the paper away, or seems tired. Ending after two minutes is a success if they enjoyed it. You can return later, or try a different picture or a different activity like [sensory play](/activities/sensory/).
