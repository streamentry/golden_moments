Bingo is a familiar, social game for many older adults, but the numbers can be hard to follow for someone living with dementia. Picture bingo keeps the same gentle rhythm and swaps the numbers for big, clear pictures. It works at home, in a day program, or in a care home. This guide covers how to play, how to adapt it, and how to keep the focus on enjoyment.

## How picture bingo works

Each player has a card with a grid of pictures, such as a teapot, a cat, a rose, a sailboat or a bicycle. A caller shows or names one picture at a time. Players mark the matching picture on their card. A player who marks a full row, or the set the group agrees on, can call out "Bingo," but many groups skip the winner entirely.

You can use the printable cards from our [free printable activities](/activities/printable/), or the [picture bingo game](/games/picture-bingo/), which includes an on-screen caller for groups. Both work well on a tablet held up for the whole group to see.

## Setting up a session

- **Choose a small group.** Four to eight people is a good size. Larger groups are harder to follow and can feel rushed.
- **Make the pictures large.** Use bold lines and one familiar item per square. Avoid crowded cards.
- **Seat people well.** Put each player where they can see the caller and their own card, and near a helper if they need one.
- **Use simple markers.** Large chips, coins, or a finger tap work better than pencils. Keep a few spares.
- **Plan a short session.** Thirty minutes is often plenty. Stop early if energy drops.

The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-caregiving/adapting-activities-people-alzheimers-disease) advises adapting activities to what a person can still do, so simplify the cards and the rules whenever that helps.

## How to call the pictures

1. **Hold the picture up** or point to it on the screen before you speak.
2. **Name it clearly,** in plain words: "Teapot." Repeat it once, slowly.
3. **Wait.** Give people time to look and mark. Do not call the next picture until most people have had a chance.
4. **Describe, if it helps.** "The red flower" or "the one we had at the picnic" can spark talk.
5. **Keep the pace gentle.** A calm caller is more important than a fast one.

## Making winning optional

Competition can be a problem. A person who is losing may feel tested, and a person who is anxious about winning may become tense. Here are some alternatives:

- **Everyone wins a picture.** Each player calls out one picture they like, and the group admires it.
- **Share a full card.** Ask players to say something about one picture on their card.
- **Play until the picture runs out.** Call every picture on a card, then start another, with no claim on a first winner.
- **Keep the prize small or skip it.** A cookie or a song works better than a trophy.

## Adapting for people in later stages

For people who find finding a picture hard, use a card with only four or six squares, or give each player one card at a time. Let a helper hold the card while the person points, or gently move the chip with their permission. Some people find it easier just to listen to the caller and enjoy the sound of the words.

Watch for signs of discomfort, such as pulling away or frowning, and stop if they appear. For more ideas about quiet, sit-down activities, see [late-stage activities](/activities/late-stage/).

## Bingo in care homes and day programs

Group activities work best when they are planned around the people in the room. Our [group activities guide for care homes and day programs](/activities/care-home-groups/) covers how to plan a session, seat people, and handle people who want to leave. Picture bingo also pairs well with a sing-along or a story, and with [finish-the-saying](/activities/finish-the-saying/) as a quieter game later in the session.

## What bingo can and cannot do

Picture bingo is sociable and enjoyable. It can bring people together, give a reason to look at pictures and listen, and make a group feel warm. It does not treat dementia or slow its course. Judge its success by how people look and feel during the hour, not by who wins.
