Reminiscence is the practice of talking together about the past: childhood homes, first jobs, favorite foods, songs, weddings and holidays. For many people with dementia, a warm conversation about their own life is one of the most enjoyable parts of the day. The key is to ask in a way that invites a story and never tests a memory. These questions are grouped by theme, with tips on how to use them.

## Rules for reminiscence questions

- **Ask open questions.** "Tell me about..." and "What was it like..." invite a story. "Do you remember...?" asks for a right answer.
- **Never quiz.** Avoid "Who is this?", "What year was that?" and "Do you recall the name of...?"
- **Accept whatever comes back.** If the story changes or places get mixed up, enjoy it and ask about the feelings.
- **Use one question at a time.** Wait, listen and follow up with something simple.
- **Let the person lead.** If they want to talk about something else, go with them.

The [National Institute on Aging's guidance on communicating](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/alzheimers-changes-behavior-and-communication/dos-and-donts-communicating-person-who-has) recommends short sentences, a calm voice, and patience while the person finds their words. These rules matter as much here as anywhere.

## Home and childhood

- Tell me about the house you grew up in. What was the kitchen like?
- What did you like to do outside when you were little?
- Who were the people you played with on your street?
- What was your bedroom like? Did you share it with anyone?

## Work and getting things done

- What kind of work did you enjoy most?
- Tell me about a day at work that made you proud.
- What did you like to make or fix with your hands?
- Who taught you the most about your work?

## Food and cooking

- What did your family eat on a Sunday?
- Tell me about a dish someone in your family made especially well.
- Did you have a favorite place to get a treat when you were young?
- What smells remind you of home?

Food talk pairs nicely with the [sensory activities](/activities/sensory/) guide, and with a small taste if it is safe to offer one.

## Music and dancing

- What songs did you sing or dance to when you were young?
- Who was your favorite singer or band?
- Where did you hear music in those days: at home, at dances, at church?

For playlists and music ideas, see [music activities for people with dementia](/activities/music/).

## Holidays and celebrations

- What was your favorite holiday growing up?
- Tell me about a birthday party you remember.
- What did the family do on a summer evening?
- How did your family celebrate the seasons?

For ideas that suit a particular holiday, see [holiday activities for someone with dementia](/activities/holidays/).

## Family and friends

- Tell me about the person who made you laugh the most.
- What was your wedding day like?
- Tell me about your children when they were small.
- What did your friends call you?

Ask family questions gently. If the person is grieving someone, follow their lead and avoid asking them to recall details they may find painful.

## Places and nature

- Tell me about a place you love to think about.
- What was the weather like where you grew up?
- Did you have a garden, or a favorite tree?
- Tell me about a trip you enjoyed.

## Using photos and objects

Pictures are a natural starting point. Hold a photo at eye level and say, "Tell me about this." The [Alzheimer's Association's guidance on activities](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/activities) suggests using familiar objects, photos and music to start conversation. A memory box with a few objects from the person's life can work well too; see [reminiscence activities](/activities/reminiscence/) for how to put one together.

## When the memory is painful

Some memories bring up sadness, fear or anger. If the person becomes upset, do not push. Say something warm like, "That sounds like it was a hard time. Would you like to talk about something happier?" Then change the subject, offer a cup of tea, or put on a favorite song.

## Pair with a game or a walk

After a reminiscence session, a gentle game can keep the mood light. Try [conversation cards](/games/conversation-cards/) for more open prompts, or [finish the saying](/games/finish-the-saying/) for familiar phrases from the person's childhood. For communication tips more broadly, see [communication tips for someone with dementia](/caregiving/communication-tips/).
