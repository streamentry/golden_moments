A dementia diagnosis can bring questions about health, daily life, support and the future. People respond in different ways: some want time to absorb the news, while others want to start planning. There is no single right reaction or timetable. Begin with what matters to the person now, and return to other questions when they are ready.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to life after diagnosis](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/diagnosis/life-after-diagnosis) points people toward follow-up care, local support and future planning. [Dementia Australia's guidance on coping with a diagnosis](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/what-next-after-your-diagnosis/coping-your-diagnosis) emphasizes that people react differently and can seek support when they want it. This page is a starting point, not a personal medical, legal or financial plan.

## What is one useful first step?

Choose one question that would make this week easier. It could be finding out who will coordinate the person's follow-up care, asking where to get reliable information about their diagnosis, or identifying someone they trust to talk with. The person may want to take the lead, ask someone to help or wait before deciding.

For the next appointment, write down a few questions together if the person wants. You might ask what the diagnosis means, what is still uncertain, when to follow up and whom to contact if a new concern comes up. Ask the clinician to explain any term or recommendation that is unclear. Our [doctor-visit guide](/caregiving/doctor-visit-with-dementia/) has a short preparation list.

The person does not have to learn everything about dementia at once. Choose reliable information about the diagnosis they received, and write down questions that come up later. Be cautious with promises, cure claims or advice that tells everyone to follow the same treatment or plan.

## Where can we find support?

Ask the person what kind of support would help and whom they would like involved. A trusted friend or family member may be a good first conversation. Some people prefer a support group, a counselor, a local dementia organization or a conversation with their care team. The right option depends on the person and what is available nearby.

Support is for the person with the diagnosis as well as family and friends. A caregiver might need respite or help with daily tasks; the person may want information or a chance to meet others living with dementia. These needs can differ. Ask before arranging a group, making an appointment or sharing personal details.

Our [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) has ideas for asking others to share support. If the person would like more help during the day, the guides to [adult day programs](/caregiving/choosing-adult-day-program-dementia/) and [in-home care](/caregiving/choosing-in-home-dementia-care/) explain questions to ask about local services.

## How can the person stay part of decisions?

A diagnosis alone does not tell you what a person can decide or how they want to take part. Ask the person directly. Give them time, explain the choice in plain language and ask what support would make the conversation easier. Some choices may be easier to discuss in a quiet setting or across more than one conversation.

If the person wants to plan for future health care, finances or living arrangements, start with what matters to them and who they trust. The [Dementia Australia planning guide](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/what-next-after-your-diagnosis/planning-ahead) recommends discussing wishes with trusted people and relevant professionals. Legal documents and decision-making rules differ by location, so ask a qualified local professional before relying on a general guide. For everyday bill help and scam concerns, see our [guide to managing money with dementia](/caregiving/managing-money-with-dementia/).

### What can we ask in a first conversation about future care?

Ask if the person wants to talk now, then bring up one topic at a time. The [Alzheimer's Association recommends several small conversations, plain language and listening for the person's wishes, beliefs and values](https://www.alz.org/professionals/health-systems-medical-professionals/management/advanced-care-planning). The [Alzheimer's Society notes that people differ in how ready they feel to think ahead](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/living-with-dementia/planning-ahead). It is fine to pause and return another day.

If the person wants prompts, try one of these:

- "What parts of your day would you most want to keep?"
- "Who would you like included when we talk about future care?"
- "What helps you feel comfortable and respected when you need help?"
- "Is there anything you want your care team to know about what matters to you?"
- "Would you like me to write down what you said, and who should see it?"

Listen without asking for a decision right away. The [National Institute on Aging describes meaningful conversations about values and preferences as central to advance care planning](https://order.nia.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2024-03/caring-for-a-Person-with-alzheimers-disease.pdf). If the person agrees, note their words, the date and who was there. Ask permission before sharing the note. These notes can guide later conversations, but they are not legal documents or medical orders.

Do not make assumptions based only on the diagnosis or on one conversation. The person's preferences may change, and they can ask to pause or return to a topic later. Keep them involved in decisions that affect them as much as they want.

## Who should know about the diagnosis?

The person decides whether, when and how to tell other people, unless a specific safety or legal situation requires professional advice. Ask whom they want to tell and what they want those people to know. They may choose one trusted person first, or prefer not to share widely.

If the person wants help with a conversation, ask what they would like you to say and whether they want to be there. Share only the information they have agreed to share. Our [communication guide](/caregiving/communication-tips/) covers respectful conversations with family and others.

## How can daily life continue while we adjust?

Keep the routines and activities that matter to the person where they still feel comfortable. A diagnosis does not mean every hobby, outing or household role has to stop. If something becomes harder, ask what the person would like to change. A shorter visit, familiar music, help with one step or a different time of day may suit them better.

The person may also want space to process the news without discussing plans. Keep checking in without turning every conversation into a care meeting. It is okay to take a break from planning and spend time together in an ordinary way.

## When should we ask a professional?

Ask the person's clinician about questions specific to their diagnosis, health, medicines or symptoms. For legal and financial decisions, speak with qualified professionals who understand the rules where the person lives. A general website cannot tell you which treatment, document or service is right for a particular person.

Sudden confusion or another abrupt change in health needs urgent medical attention. The [NHS guidance on sudden confusion](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/) says to get medical help immediately. Use the local emergency or urgent-care system where the person lives; do not assume a sudden change is part of their usual condition.

If planning feels overwhelming, choose one trusted person or service to help sort questions into immediate and later topics. The person can set the pace and decide what they want help with. A diagnosis is important information, but it does not define the person's interests, relationships or daily life.
