Considering residential care can bring relief, grief, uncertainty or several feelings at once. The decision is about finding support that fits the person's needs and preferences. It is not a test of how much a family cares or a fixed step that everyone reaches at the same time.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to long-term care](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-term-care), its [residential-care checklist](https://www.alz.org/getmedia/2f238875-3f5d-447e-adcc-dd9fc07f828b/alzheimers-dementia-choosing-residential-care-ts.pdf) and [Dementia Australia's residential-care guidance](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/care-options/residential-care) suggest looking at the person's needs, staff, activities, environment and costs. This page does not recommend a particular home or replace local professional and financial advice.

## How can I tell whether residential care is worth exploring?

Look at what support the person needs now and whether that support can be provided at home. Consider the person's safety, daily care needs, social life and preferences. Also consider whether caregiving demands are affecting the health of the person or the people supporting them.

There is no single stage or checklist that decides when someone should move. A person may prefer a community setting, or may want to stay at home with added help. Talk with the person and, if useful, their doctor, social worker or care coordinator. Ask what types of support might fit before committing to a move.

Include the person in decisions as much as they want. Ask what matters to them: location, meals, language, culture, religious practice, familiar activities or visits from family and friends. Their preferences can help narrow the list even when some care needs are changing.

## What should I find out before visiting homes?

First, make a short list of the support the person needs. This may include help with daily activities, nursing, social connection, a familiar language or a place to spend time outdoors. Ask a health or social-care professional which services may be needed if you are unsure.

Call homes before visiting. Ask whether they can support the person's needs, whether a room may be available, what the next steps are and what information they need from you. Request written information about the services and fees. Eligibility, funding and licensing rules vary by location, so check local sources rather than relying on a general article.

Ask whether staff have dementia-care training, how care plans are made and how families can share updates. Find out how daily routines work, what activities are offered and how residents are supported to make choices. A memory-care unit is one option, but not everyone needs one; compare the actual support each place provides.

## What should I notice during a visit?

Visit more than one home if possible. Make an appointment for an initial tour, then ask whether you can visit again at a different time or during a meal. A second visit can show more of the usual routine and allow you to meet other staff.

Notice how staff speak with residents. Do they address people respectfully, give time to answer and involve them in ordinary choices? Do residents appear comfortable and have things to do that fit their interests and abilities? Ask if you can see activity schedules, dining areas, outdoor spaces and private areas.

Look for the details that matter to this person: signs that are easy to understand, clear routes to the bathroom, familiar belongings, quiet spaces, language and cultural support, and opportunities to see loved ones. A polished lobby alone does not show how daily care works.

Bring a trusted friend or relative if that would help you remember questions and compare impressions afterward. You can use the [Alzheimer's Association checklist](https://www.alz.org/getmedia/2f238875-3f5d-447e-adcc-dd9fc07f828b/alzheimers-dementia-choosing-residential-care-ts.pdf) to note what you see. Treat your notes as prompts for discussion, not as a score that chooses the home for you.

## What questions help compare the care?

Ask how staffing works across different times of day, how staff learn the person's routines and what happens when their needs change. Ask who reviews care plans and how the family can raise a concern. If the person needs help with meals, personal care or communication, ask what that support looks like in practice.

Ask about family visits, outings, medical appointments, activities and the person's ability to bring familiar items. If a home has a dementia-specific program, ask what training, activities and daily support it includes. Ask for examples of how residents are involved in choices rather than relying only on the program name.

Ask for an itemized description of fees, what is included and how charges may change. Read agreements carefully and get qualified local advice if financial or legal terms are unclear. Do not assume that a service is covered or available based on information from another state or country.

## How can I help with the move?

Involve the person in choices as much as they want. A visit to the home, a meal there or a short respite stay may help some people get familiar with the setting, if available and acceptable to them. The person may prefer to choose a room item, clothing or routine to bring along.

Share useful information with staff, with the person's agreement. This could include their preferred name, communication style, daily routines, foods they like, activities they enjoy and what helps them feel settled. Keep the information brief and focused on care. The person should remain part of conversations about them.

Bring familiar belongings if the home allows them. Ask the staff how the person's room can feel recognizable without making it hard to move around. A photograph, favorite blanket or familiar clock may matter more than trying to recreate the whole home.

## How can I stay involved after the move?

Work with the staff to learn how updates are shared and who to contact. Ask the person how they feel about visits and activities. Some people may want frequent short visits; others may prefer time to settle. Notice what seems comfortable to them and adjust together.

Care needs and preferences can change. Review the care plan with the person and staff, ask questions when something is unclear and share new information that may help. If the person suddenly becomes confused or much more unwell, tell the care team promptly; sudden confusion needs immediate medical attention, according to the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/).

Choosing a home is a serious decision, and a first choice may not remain the best fit. Gather information, include the person, ask about daily care and keep communication open after the move.
