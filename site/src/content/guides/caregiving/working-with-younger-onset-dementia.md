A dementia diagnosis during working life can raise questions about income, privacy, identity and safety. Some people want to keep their current job, some consider changes, and others choose to leave. There is no single path that fits everyone. Your preferences, work tasks, health and support all matter.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to younger-onset Alzheimer's](https://www.alz.org/help-support/i-have-alz/younger-onset) discusses work, family finances and employer benefits. [Dementia Australia](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/what-next-after-your-diagnosis/planning-ahead/work-and-dementia) and [Alzheimer's Society](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/living-with-dementia/work-dementia) describe options for staying at work, changing a role or leaving. Employment rules differ by location, so this guide offers questions to consider, not legal or financial advice.

## Does a diagnosis mean I have to stop working?

No. A diagnosis by itself does not decide whether you can keep working. The [Alzheimer's Society says](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/living-with-dementia/work-dementia) the decision depends on your feelings, your job and your personal situation. Some people continue in the same role, some change tasks or hours, and some leave work.

Think about what is working and what has become harder. You could note which tasks feel familiar, when you feel tired or distracted, and whether there have been specific mistakes or safety concerns. Concrete examples can help you and your clinician discuss the job without turning one difficult day into a conclusion about your ability.

Safety matters too. If your job involves driving, operating machinery, caring for other people or another safety-sensitive task, ask your clinician how to assess the risk. Local laws, professional rules and employment contracts may also apply. This page cannot tell you whether a specific role is safe or what your employer must do.

## How can I talk with my clinician about work?

Describe the tasks your job involves and the changes you have noticed. Ask whether a health concern could affect a specific duty, what signs mean you should seek help, and whether another professional can assess a work task. You may want to bring a trusted person, but decide together how they can support you without speaking over you.

The [doctor-visit guide](/caregiving/doctor-visit-with-dementia/) has ways to prepare questions and notes. You can ask your clinician to explain what they can assess and what needs a separate workplace or occupational evaluation. A general dementia guide cannot replace an individual health and safety assessment.

## What changes at work might help?

If you want to keep working, ask what changes may be possible in your role. Dementia Australia suggests options such as using a routine, focusing on one task at a time, taking notes and making a step-by-step guide for common tasks. Depending on your job, you might also ask about written instructions, a calendar, fewer interruptions or planned breaks.

Treat these as ideas to discuss, not a checklist that every employer can or should use. Choose one change that addresses a task you find difficult. If you and your employer try it, agree how you will review it and whether it helps. A change that works for one role may not suit another.

## Should I tell my employer?

You decide what personal information to share, unless local law, a contract or a safety requirement applies to your role. Requirements differ by country, state and job. Before deciding, check your contract, workplace policy and local guidance. If you are unsure, talk with a qualified employment adviser, union representative or lawyer who knows the rules where you live.

If you choose to tell someone, consider what you want them to know and what support you are asking for. You may prefer to speak with one trusted manager or HR contact first. Ask how your information will be handled and who will receive it; do not assume every workplace has the same privacy process.

Some jobs involve safety-sensitive duties. If you are unsure whether to pause or change one of those duties, contact your clinician and a qualified local employment adviser promptly. Do not rely on a general guide to decide whether a specific task is safe.

## What benefits and finances should I check?

Before changing your hours or leaving, review the benefits available through your employer. Ask HR or the benefits administrator about leave, disability or income benefits, health coverage and any deadlines. Request the current rules in writing and keep a copy of the information you receive.

Eligibility, taxes and timing depend on your employer, insurance policy and location. The [Alzheimer's Association advises reviewing employer benefits and keeping written confirmation](https://www.alz.org/help-support/i-have-alz/younger-onset). For your own decision, speak with a qualified benefits or financial adviser who can review the documents that apply to you. Avoid signing or ending coverage based only on a general article.

Your family may also be affected by a change in income or schedule. If you want, talk with the people you trust about what matters most and which options you want to explore. The [guide to first steps after a diagnosis](/caregiving/after-dementia-diagnosis/) covers broader support and future-planning questions.

## What if I decide to reduce hours or leave?

Leaving work can be the right choice for some people. It does not mean you have failed. You may want to think about income, health coverage, daily routine and the people you will miss seeing. Ask about local support before you make a change, and give yourself time to compare the options that matter to you.

Work is only one part of life. If you stop or reduce hours, choose other activities or relationships that feel worthwhile when you are ready. Family members can offer help, but the decision should include you. The [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) has ideas for asking others to share support without placing every responsibility on one person.
