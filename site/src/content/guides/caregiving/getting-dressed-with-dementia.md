Getting dressed is personal. Someone living with dementia may find choices, clothing fasteners or the order of steps harder than before. They may also have a strong preference for familiar clothes or a particular style. The aim is to support comfort and choice, not to take over or make the outfit look a certain way.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to dressing](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/dressing-grooming) and [Dementia Australia's getting-dressed advice](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/home-life/getting-dressed) recommend giving enough time, keeping choices simple and supporting the person to do what they can. This page offers general ideas. Ask a doctor about new dressing difficulties or other health concerns.

## How can I offer choices without taking over?

Ask what the person would like to wear. If choosing from a full closet feels difficult, offer two familiar options. They might choose between a blue shirt and a green one, or between a sweater and a cardigan. Two is a starting point, not a rule. If the person prefers to choose from more clothes, follow their lead.

Keep the person's usual taste in view. Favorite colors, fabrics and familiar outfits can make the choice feel easier. Do not replace their style with what you think looks best. A mismatched outfit is not a reason to argue. If the person is comfortable and the clothes suit the weather, let their choice stand.

When buying clothes, consider comfort and what the person can manage. Soft fabrics, familiar shapes and closures they can open may help. Elastic or simple fasteners can be easier for some people, but a new design can also feel unfamiliar. Ask before replacing favorite clothes or changing a routine.

## What small steps make dressing easier?

Choose a time when there is room to go slowly. Rushing can make the task more stressful. A quiet, well-lit space with clothes easy to see may help. Clear other items away from the place where the person gets dressed, and put the selected clothes within reach.

Lay the clothes out in the order the person wants to put them on. Offer one item at a time and use a short cue, such as “Here is your shirt.” A broad instruction like “Get dressed” may be harder to act on. Pause between steps so the person has time to understand and respond.

Let the person do each part they can and want to do. If they are unsure what comes next, offer a reminder or ask whether help would be useful. Do not pull clothes onto them or move their body without agreement. If one step is frustrating, pause and ask what would make it easier. Sometimes trying again later is enough.

## How do I protect privacy and dignity?

Ask before staying in the room or helping with clothes. The person may be comfortable dressing alone, or may want someone nearby without hands-on help. Follow what they ask for. If help is needed, explain what you plan to do and check that they are still comfortable as you go.

Keep the person covered as much as they prefer while changing. Close the door or curtains, and avoid discussing private details where other people can hear. If a family member or care worker helps, ask whether that person is welcome. Preferences can change from one day to another, so check each time.

Reminders to change clothes can feel embarrassing. Offer a clean, familiar option without scolding or making a public correction. If the person wants to wear a favorite outfit repeatedly, similar clothes or a duplicate may help keep the choice familiar. Do not turn changing clothes into a test of memory or independence.

Our [bathing guide](/caregiving/bathing-with-dementia/) has more ideas for protecting privacy during another personal-care task.

## What clothes and shoes may feel comfortable?

Comfort depends on the person. Clothes that are soft, easy to put on and familiar may be preferable to a new outfit with many fasteners. Choose shoes the person finds comfortable and that have a good grip. The [Alzheimer's Association dressing handout](https://www.alz.org/getmedia/3e31dbc7-baf0-4f18-9c1b-7a8779af3d17/alzheimers-dementia-dressing-ts.pdf) advises checking that clothing length is suitable to reduce tripping risk.

Think about the day's temperature and plans. A person may enjoy layers, but check whether they feel too warm or cold. For time outside, bring a layer that can be added or removed. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/dressing-grooming) advises choosing comfortable shoes and considering the weather. These are prompts for a conversation with the person, not a uniform or dress code.

## What if dressing has become newly difficult?

If getting dressed has changed noticeably, ask the person what feels hard and talk with their doctor. Dementia Australia notes that vision, medication effects or another health condition may also make dressing harder. A doctor can help check possible causes. Do not stop or change a medicine based on a general article.

If the person suddenly becomes confused, seek immediate medical help through local emergency services. The [NHS guidance on sudden confusion](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/) says this needs urgent attention. This warning is about sudden confusion, not a gradual change in the dressing routine.

Notice which part of the routine has changed: choosing clothes, seeing them, handling a fastener or keeping balance. Share what you have observed with the person and their doctor, if they agree. A new difficulty does not by itself explain its cause. Avoid assuming the person is being stubborn or careless.

If you are helping at home or in a care setting, ask the person whether they want their preferences shared with another helper. A short note about favorite clothes, the order they prefer and the cue that works can help others follow the same routine. Keep the note private and update it when the person's wishes change.

The person remains in charge of their clothing as much as they want to be. Offer only the help they accept, allow time and respect the choices that feel comfortable to them.
