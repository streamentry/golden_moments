Mouth care is personal. Someone living with dementia may forget a familiar routine, find a toothbrush tiring to use or feel uncomfortable when another person offers help. Keep the routine calm and respectful. The person should do as much as they can and want to do.

The [Alzheimer's Association dental-care guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/dental-care) and [Dementia Australia's dental-care advice](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-healthy/dental-care) suggest using a familiar routine, short instructions and help from a dentist when problems arise. This page is general information, not dental advice or a care plan for a particular person.

## How can I keep daily mouth care familiar?

Use the person's usual time and place if these still work. Put the toothbrush, toothpaste and towel where the person expects them. Explain what you are doing before you start, then offer one simple cue at a time. “Pick up the toothbrush” is easier to follow than a long set of directions.

Give the person time to do each step. If they can brush on their own, let them. If a reminder is enough, wait before offering more help. Some people find it easier to sit at a sink or use a brush with a handle they can hold comfortably. Ask the dentist about options if the usual brush is difficult to manage.

Do not turn brushing into a test or a contest. If the person says no, pulls away or becomes upset, pause. Try again later, change the setting or ask the dentist for advice. Do not force a brush into someone's mouth or hold them still to finish the task.

If a routine that used to work becomes difficult, notice which step is hard. The person may be unsure what to do with the brush, dislike a new toothpaste, feel tired or have mouth discomfort. These are possibilities, not a diagnosis. A short note about when the difficulty happens can help the dentist discuss next steps. Change one part of the routine at a time so it is clear what the person prefers.

## How can I help while protecting privacy?

Ask before assisting, even if the person has needed help before. Tell them what you plan to do and wait for their response. If they agree, keep the door closed, use a towel to protect clothing and uncover only the area needed. Agree on which helper the person feels comfortable with.

If the person needs hands-on help, explain each step before doing it and stop if they show distress. A dentist can demonstrate a safe routine for the person's needs. Do not improvise physical guidance or use a technique you have not been shown by a qualified professional.

## What should I know about dentures?

Keep dentures in the person's usual, clearly identified place. Ask the dentist how they should be cleaned and stored, and whether the fit should be checked if the person seems uncomfortable. If dentures are worn in a care setting, mark the storage container so staff can identify it.

Do not force dentures into the person's mouth if they resist or seem to be in pain. A loose or uncomfortable denture can make eating harder; ask the dentist what to do. ## How can we prepare for a dental visit?

Book at a time when the person is usually rested. When the person agrees, tell the dental office in advance that they may need more time, a calm explanation or help from someone they trust. Bring a current medication list and a short note about what has become difficult, such as brushing, chewing or wearing dentures.

Ask the dental team to explain what will happen one step at a time. A familiar companion can help the person feel more settled, but the person should remain part of the conversation and decisions. If travel to a regular office is difficult, ask the dentist about accessible appointment options in your area.

## How can other helpers keep the routine consistent?

If a family member, home-care worker or care-home staff member helps, agree with the person on a routine they are comfortable with. Use the same familiar place and short cue when possible. Ask whether they prefer a particular person to help each time.

If the person agrees, share only details that will help the next helper: the preferred time, what they can do themselves, which step is difficult and when they want help to stop. Keep any written note private and follow the care setting's usual process. A written routine is a guide, not permission to continue when the person says no. Preferences can change, so ask again and adapt. If several approaches have become difficult, describe what you noticed to the dental team and ask for advice for that person.

## What signs should I mention to the dentist?

Mouth pain is not always easy to describe. Pulling at the face, refusing to brush, changes in eating or disturbed sleep may be clues, but they do not prove a dental problem. Tell the dentist what you have noticed and when it started. The dentist can check for pain, gum problems, tooth decay or dentures that no longer fit well.

If someone has a new problem chewing or swallowing, contact their health professional or dentist. Do not change food texture, use mouth treatments or try swallowing techniques based on a general article. Seek urgent help for severe pain, swelling, bleeding or another immediate medical concern.

The aim is a routine the person can accept, with the least help needed. A familiar reminder, a comfortable position or a later attempt may be enough. If the problem continues, ask the dentist what support fits the person.
