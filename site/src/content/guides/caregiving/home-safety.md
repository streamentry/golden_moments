A home safety change should solve a problem the person has actually faced, not turn every room into a list of restrictions. Start with one room and one concern: a dark route to the bathroom, a cable across a walkway, or uncertainty around a kitchen appliance. The goal is to make everyday routines easier to continue.

The [Alzheimer's Association home safety checklist](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/safety/home-safety) covers risks in kitchens, bathrooms, bedrooms, storage areas and outside spaces. It also says changes should support the person's needs and independence. The [Alzheimer's Society's home equipment guide](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/living-with-dementia/what-equipment-improve-adapt-home-person-dementia) explains how small changes can make daily routines easier and safer. Neither list fits every person or home.

## How should I start a home safety review?

Choose one room where something has already gone wrong or feels difficult. If the person wants to take part, walk through together and ask what feels hard. A caregiver may notice a loose cable; the person may point out that a light switch is difficult to reach or a familiar chair has moved. Both views help you choose a change that makes sense.

Write down the problem in plain words: “The hallway is dark after dinner,” “The bathroom door is hard to find at night,” or “The cleaner is kept beside the sink.” Then choose one practical step and see whether it helps. You do not need to buy equipment for every concern. Better lighting, a cleared path or moving a frequently used item within easy reach may be enough.

The [Alzheimer's Society's home safety tips](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/publications-and-factsheets/dementia-together/tips-staying-safe-secure-home-dementia) include examples shared by people affected by dementia, such as keeping useful information easy to find and using sensor lights at night. Treat these as ideas to consider, not instructions that everyone should follow.

## What should I check in each room?

### Entrances, hallways and stairs

- **Make the route easy to see.** Check that entries, stairs and the path between the bedroom and bathroom have steady light. A night light or a motion-sensor light may help if the person is often up after dark.
- **Clear walking paths.** Move loose cables, clutter and items that are easy to trip over. Check that rugs lie flat; remove or secure one only if it is causing a problem.
- **Look at changes in floor level.** If a step or threshold is hard to notice, ask an occupational therapist or home-safety professional which change would improve visibility without creating another edge to trip on.
- **Keep familiar items where they make sense.** A chair, lamp or commonly used object may help someone follow a routine. Moving everything for a tidier look can make the route less familiar.

### Kitchen

- **Review appliance use.** If there has been a problem with the stove or another appliance, look into safety options designed for that specific appliance. The Alzheimer's Association lists automatic shut-off features and stove-safety devices as possibilities. Get qualified advice before changing gas or electrical equipment.
- **Keep medicines and non-food items apart from food.** The Association recommends storing medicines safely rather than leaving them on a counter. If the person manages their own medicines, ask a pharmacist or health professional for a plan that protects that routine.
- **Store cleaners and hazardous products safely.** Keep them in their original containers, away from food and out of reach when needed. Do not transfer chemicals into a drink bottle or food jar.
- **Keep useful kitchen items accessible.** A safe arrangement should let the person continue tasks they enjoy when possible. If you change a cupboard or appliance, explain the change and check whether it creates confusion.

If the concern comes up during a shared chore, our [household tasks guide](/activities/household-tasks/) has ideas for adapting the job while keeping the person involved.

### Bathroom

- **Light the route and the room.** Check the switch, night lighting and path to the toilet. The Alzheimer's Society suggests a night light or sensor light when it helps someone find the room.
- **Check slippery areas and supports.** If the floor or tub is slippery, ask a qualified professional about a suitable non-slip surface, grab rail or other support. The right placement depends on the person and the bathroom.
- **Keep the room easy to recognize and use.** Put everyday items where the person expects them. If they have trouble finding the toilet, a simple sign or keeping the door open may help, if it suits the home.
- **Do not bring a portable heater into the bathroom.** Keep electrical equipment away from wet areas and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

### Bedroom and night-time route

- **Clear the way from bed to the bathroom.** Remove objects from the floor and make sure the person can reach a light without crossing a dark room.
- **Check heaters and warming devices.** If an electric blanket, heating pad or portable heater is in use, review its placement and instructions. Ask for advice if there is a risk of burns or the person cannot use it safely alone.
- **Make everyday items reachable.** Keep clothing, glasses, a phone or other needed items in familiar places and at a height the person can reach without climbing.

### Laundry, storage and outdoor areas

- **Secure cleaning products, medicines and tools.** Check cupboards, laundry areas, garages, sheds and basements. Store chemicals in their original containers and keep sharp tools or machinery secured when there is a specific risk.
- **Check appliances and alarms.** Make sure smoke alarms and carbon-monoxide detectors are present and working. Follow their instructions for testing and battery replacement.
- **Keep an emergency contact list easy to find.** Include the local emergency number, the person's key contacts and any information a trusted helper may need. Do not put private details where a visitor can see them.
- **Check the path outside.** Look for poor lighting, uneven surfaces, hoses or tools in a walkway. Make changes one at a time so familiar routes remain recognizable.

For night-time routines, the [sleep guide](/brain-health/sleep/) has more on sleep changes and familiar evening habits.

## How can I keep changes respectful?

Safety and independence need to work together. If a change stops someone from using a room or doing a familiar task, pause and look for a less restrictive option. Some organizations list locks for specific risks; that is not a blanket instruction. Before changing a door or limiting a route, get local home- and fire-safety advice and consider how the person will leave in an emergency. Keep a safe exit available.

When possible, explain a change before making it and give the person time to get used to it. The Alzheimer's Society advises thinking about whether equipment will be safe to use alone, whether it needs installation, and whether it might make another household task harder. Ask the person what they notice after the change. If they avoid the room or stop using something they value, adjust the plan.

Revisit the home when routines, movement or vision change, or after a near miss. A room that worked well last month may need a different light or clearer route now. You can keep a short list of what has changed and what still works, rather than trying to redesign the whole home at once.

## Who can help with home adaptations?

An occupational therapist can assess how a person moves through the home and suggest equipment or changes for everyday tasks. Depending on the concern, a physiotherapist, district nurse, pharmacist or housing professional may also help. The Alzheimer's Society notes that an occupational therapist may be available through a GP, hospital or local social-care service in the UK; routes differ elsewhere. For an installation or change to gas, electrical, structural or fire-safety equipment, use a qualified local professional.

If you rent, ask the landlord or housing provider before making permanent changes. A local aging or disability service may explain what assessments or support are available. If a problem is urgent, deal with that specific hazard first and ask for professional help with anything you are unsure how to change safely.

## How often should I review the home?

Review it when a familiar task becomes harder, movement or vision changes, or there is a near miss. Start with the room or routine connected to that change. You do not need to complete every item on a checklist; keep what is working and revisit one practical change at a time.
