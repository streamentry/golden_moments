Living alone does not, by itself, say how much help a person needs. The Alzheimer's Association notes that many people with Alzheimer's continue living on their own, particularly in the early stage, with simple adjustments, safety precautions and support from others. The useful question is more specific: what does this person want to keep doing, and what would make those tasks easier?

Start that conversation when there is time and no immediate problem to solve. Ask what feels comfortable, which routines matter and what kind of help the person would welcome. A plan that is made with them is more likely to fit their life than a list of tasks arranged around someone else's worries.

## What can make day-to-day life easier?

Use a few familiar supports that match the person's routines. The [Alzheimer's Association recommends practical aids](https://www.alz.org/help-support/i-have-alz/know-what-to-expect/if_you_live_alone) such as calendars, lists and reminders for daily needs.

- **Choose one place for essentials.** Keep keys, glasses, wallet and phone in the same agreed place. A tray near the door or a labeled shelf can make the routine easier to follow.
- **Use one calendar or reminder system.** Pick a wall calendar, whiteboard, paper diary or phone calendar that the person already likes using. Keep appointments and regular activities in one place rather than spreading them across several apps and notes.
- **Make routines visible.** If a task is often forgotten, write a short note or put the item needed for it in a familiar place. Keep the steps brief and review them together if they stop helping.
- **Make recurring chores predictable.** A regular shopping delivery, housekeeping visit or meal arrangement can reduce the number of decisions the person has to manage alone.

Our guide to [daily routines](/activities/daily-routine/) has simple ways to keep a day familiar without making it rigid. The goal is to support the person's choices, not to fill every hour.

## How can family and friends offer useful help?

Agree on a small number of regular contacts. A short visit or call at a known time can be more useful than several people checking in unpredictably. Ask the person who they want involved and what each person can do.

Make offers concrete. “Would it help if I brought groceries on Thursday?” is easier to answer than “Let me know if you need anything.” A trusted person might help with shopping, transport, meals, bills or an appointment calendar. The [Alzheimer's Association suggests arranging help](https://www.alz.org/help-support/i-have-alz/know-what-to-expect/if_you_live_alone) with household tasks, meals, transportation and medication reminders when those needs arise.

If several people are helping, agree who will handle each task and how they will pass along important updates. Keep information limited to the people the person has chosen, except when an urgent safety concern requires immediate help. The [communication guide](/caregiving/communication-tips/) has more on discussing support without turning it into a test.

## How can someone stay connected while living alone?

Choose activities and contact that the person enjoys. It could be a weekly call, a neighbor's visit, a community meal, a familiar club or a walk with someone they trust. Ask the person which options feel welcome; do not assume that more visitors or groups are always better.

Regular social time can make the week easier to recognize and give the person something to look forward to. If travel to an activity is becoming difficult, consider a call, a ride from a trusted person or a smaller event closer to home. Our guide to [social connection](/brain-health/social-connection/) includes low-pressure ways to keep in touch.

## How do we notice when the plan needs to change?

There is no one checklist that decides whether someone can live alone. Instead, pay attention to specific changes in daily life: a task the person handled comfortably now causes repeated trouble, meals are being missed, appointments are getting lost, or they say that a particular job feels harder.

Ask the person what they have noticed. Offer one practical change and check whether it helps. If there are repeated concerns about meals, medicines, finances, mobility or home safety, ask the person whether they would speak with their doctor or a social-care professional. A formal assessment can help identify support options; it does not require the person to accept every suggestion.

If the person seems unsure about help, start small. They may prefer a weekly grocery delivery over a caregiver in the home, or help with paperwork but not personal care. Include them in choosing the person who will help and when visits happen. The [home safety guide](/caregiving/home-safety/) covers room-by-room changes when the concern is about the home itself.

## What if the person does not want help?

Listen to the reason before offering another plan. They may worry about privacy, cost, losing control or having an unfamiliar person in the home. Ask what kind of help would feel acceptable, then see whether a small trial works. Some people prefer a reminder or a shared task; others want support from one trusted person rather than a new service.

Avoid turning check-ins into tests. A call can be a conversation, not a list of questions about whether the person ate, took medicine or remembered an appointment. If there is an immediate danger, contact local emergency services. For ongoing concerns, keep the person involved and seek advice from their care team or local support service.

Living alone may continue to work with a changing mix of routines, relationships and practical help. Review the plan with the person, keep what feels useful and adjust the parts that no longer fit.
