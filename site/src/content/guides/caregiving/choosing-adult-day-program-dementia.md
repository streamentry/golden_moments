An adult day program can offer a change of scene and support for daily routines. It may also give a family caregiver time for work, errands or rest. Programs differ, and a center that suits one person may not suit another. The person's comfort and wishes should guide the decision.

The [Alzheimer's Association overview of adult day centers](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/adult-day-centers) and its [checklist for choosing a center](https://www.alz.org/media/Documents/alzheimers-dementia-choosing-an-adult-day-center-ts.pdf) recommend checking services, staff, activities, cost and transport before enrolling. This page helps you prepare questions; it does not rate or recommend a specific center.

## How can I decide whether to explore a day program?

Start by asking the person what they might enjoy and what kind of help they would accept. They may want a social activity, a meal with others, company while a caregiver works, or something else. A program is an option, not a requirement.

Think about the trips the person wants to make, their usual routines and what support they need during the day. Consider whether the center's hours, transportation and activities fit those needs. If the person dislikes groups or becomes tired in a busy setting, ask whether there is a quieter option or a shorter visit.

The program may also help a caregiver rest or manage other responsibilities. That matters, but the person's experience matters too. Discuss both needs openly and look for an arrangement that feels acceptable to everyone involved.

## What services should I ask about?

Services vary. Some centers focus on companionship and activities; others may also offer meals, transportation, personal care or health-related services. Ask which services are available on the days the person would attend and whether staff can support the needs the person has now.

Find out about activities, not just the schedule title. Ask what participants actually do, whether they can choose among options and whether the program includes familiar interests such as music, art, gardening or conversation. The person does not need to join every activity. Ask whether quiet space or time to rest is available.

If the person needs help with medication, mobility, eating or personal care, ask exactly who provides that support and what training they have. Do not assume that a day program offers medical care or can handle every need. Confirm any health-related instructions with the person's care team.

## What can I look for during a visit?

Arrange a visit and, if the person wants, bring them along. Notice whether staff speak respectfully, give participants time to respond and invite people to take part without pressure. Look for activities that match the person's interests and abilities, not only a busy calendar.

Ask how the center learns about each person's preferences and how it responds if someone becomes tired, worried or wants to leave. Find out what the center does in an emergency, how family members receive updates and who to contact with a concern.

If possible, visit during an activity or meal. Notice whether the space feels comfortable to the person, whether it is easy to move around and whether staff seem available. A brochure or tour can help you prepare questions, but seeing an ordinary day may tell you more.

## What practical details should I check?

Ask about hours, days, fees, minimum attendance, cancellation rules and any extra charges. Ask what meals or snacks are included and whether a special diet can be supported. Costs and services vary by location, so ask the center to explain its written terms.

Find out whether transportation is available, what it costs and whether it suits the person. Ask about pickup times, assistance getting on and off, and what happens if the driver is delayed. If a family member will provide rides, include that time and cost in your plan.

Ask whether the center can provide the support the person needs if their health or daily abilities change. If the center cannot meet those needs, ask what referral or alternative it suggests. The [in-home care guide](/caregiving/choosing-in-home-dementia-care/) covers another kind of support that may fit some families.

## How can the person try a program without pressure?

Ask whether the center offers a short introductory visit, meal or activity. Let the person choose what to try and how long to stay when possible. A first visit may feel unfamiliar. Ask the person afterward what they liked, disliked or would change.

If the person is hesitant, find out why. The ride, noise, unfamiliar people or a certain activity may be the concern. You could ask the center whether a smaller adjustment is possible, or visit another day. Do not use guilt or insistence to make the person attend.

After a few visits, talk with the person and center staff about how the arrangement is working. A good program may need adjustments to timing, activities or support. If the person remains distressed or the service does not fit, it is reasonable to reconsider.

## How can I coordinate with the center?

With the person's agreement, share information that helps staff support them: their preferred name, communication style, routines, interests and the kind of help they accept. Ask how the center keeps this information private and how updates are shared with family.

Ask who to contact if the person is ill, absent or needs a change in support. Keep a backup plan for days when the program is closed or transportation is unavailable. If several people help, agree who will handle each ride and update.

An adult day program can be one part of a wider support plan. The [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) includes ways to arrange breaks, and [activities for care-home groups](/activities/care-home-groups/) offers ideas for simple group activities.

The right program is one that the person wants to try and that can support their needs. Visit, ask direct questions and make changes based on how the person feels.
