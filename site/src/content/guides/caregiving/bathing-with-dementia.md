Bathing is private. For someone living with dementia, a shower or bath may also feel cold, exposing, confusing or unsafe. Begin by asking what would make the task more comfortable, then prepare the room before offering help. The goal is to support the person's own routine without turning a refusal into a struggle.

The [Alzheimer's Association bathing guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/bathing) notes that receiving help with an intimate task can feel uncomfortable. [Dementia Australia's hygiene advice](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-healthy/hygiene) recommends following the person's preferences, using simple steps and asking what support they want. People differ; try only changes that suit this person.

## How should I prepare before offering help?

Ask about the person's usual routine. Do they prefer a shower or bath, morning or evening, help from one particular person, or to wash some parts themselves? Follow familiar preferences where you can. If they want a choice, offer two clear options, such as “Would you like a shower now or after breakfast?”

Before inviting them into the bathroom:

- **Gather supplies first.** Put out towels, clean clothes, soap, shampoo and a washcloth in the order they will be used. This avoids leaving the person alone while you search for things.
- **Make the room comfortable.** Check that it is warm enough, light the space, close the door or curtains, and keep the floor as dry as possible.
- **Check the water.** Test the temperature before the person gets into the bath or shower. Do not rely on their ability to judge whether water is too hot.
- **Have privacy items ready.** A large towel, robe or bath blanket can help the person stay covered while moving between steps.

Our [home safety guide](/caregiving/home-safety/) has more on bathroom lighting, slippery surfaces and asking a qualified professional about grab rails or a shower seat.

## How can I help without taking over?

Ask permission before touching the person or helping with a private area. Explain what you are about to do in a short sentence, then pause for a response. A calm cue such as “Here is the washcloth” is clearer than a long list of instructions.

Let the person wash the parts they can and want to wash. You can keep a towel over them and uncover only the area being washed. If they prefer, give them a washcloth or soap to hold. The person may need more time to begin or finish; wait rather than rushing through the steps.

If the person becomes upset, pulls away or says no, stop. Give them space and try to understand what is uncomfortable. The room may be cold, the water may feel strange, or the person may want a different helper. A familiar person may feel easier if the person chooses them. Do not use surprise, force or physical guidance to finish the bath.

## How can I keep the routine familiar?

Keep the usual time of day and order of steps when they work well. Put familiar soap and towels where the person expects them, and lay out clean clothes before starting. Explain one step at a time; if a spoken instruction is not clear, show the washcloth or point to the next item instead of repeating a longer explanation. If washing hair causes distress, do it separately or leave it for another day. These small changes can make the task easier to follow without taking away the person's role.

## What if a full bath or shower feels too difficult?

A full bath is not the only way to help someone feel clean. If the person is comfortable with it, try washing at the sink, using a warm washcloth or washing one area at a time. Hair washing can happen on another day if that is the part they dislike. Keep the person covered and involved, and stop if the alternative also causes distress.

If the person refuses today, accept it and try again later. A calm wash tomorrow is better than making today's attempt frightening. The [guide to when someone says no](/caregiving/when-they-say-no/) has more ways to offer help without pressure.

## How can I make the bathroom safer?

Keep the way to the bath or shower clear and use supports suited to the person's needs. Non-slip surfaces, a stable seat or grab rails may help, but equipment needs the right placement. Ask an occupational therapist or another qualified professional before installing rails or making structural changes. Do not bring a portable heater into a bathroom, and follow the instructions for any electrical equipment.

If the person is unsteady or has fallen, do not try to lift or move them in a way that could hurt either of you. Seek professional advice about the equipment and assistance needed. Keep any established safety plan consistent with the person's right to privacy and choice.

## When should I ask for professional advice?

Speak with a health professional if bathing becomes difficult because of new pain, a fall, a skin concern or a change in the person's ability to wash safely. A dentist, doctor, nurse or occupational therapist may be able to help with a specific problem. Do not use a general online guide to decide whether a person needs hands-on care.

If the person has been unable to wash for a while, avoid blaming them. Ask what part is hardest and whether a smaller change would help. A familiar routine, a washcloth or help from a trusted person may be enough; if not, ask the care team for support.
