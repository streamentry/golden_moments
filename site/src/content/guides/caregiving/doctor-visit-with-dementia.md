A doctor visit can cover a lot in a short time. A brief plan can help the person and their supporter raise the questions that matter most, understand the answers and leave with clear next steps. The person living with dementia should stay part of the conversation and decisions as much as they want.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to working with the doctor](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/working-with-the-doctor) recommends writing questions down, noting specific changes and asking the doctor to recap the plan. [Dementia Australia's advice on working with doctors](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-healthy/working-doctors) emphasizes consent, listening to the person and sharing information discreetly when needed. This page offers general preparation tips, not medical advice.

## How can I prepare before the appointment?

Ask the person what they most want the doctor to know. Write a short list of questions together, and put the most important one first. If the list is long, ask the office whether a longer appointment or a follow-up visit would help. The person may prefer to bring a written note rather than remember everything during the visit.

Describe changes in concrete terms. Note what happened, when it started, how often it occurs and what was happening at the time. For example, “She has stopped going to the weekly music group over the last month” is more useful than “She is getting worse.” Include the person's own view of the change, and do not present a guess as a diagnosis.

Bring a current list of medications, including prescription and non-prescription medicines, vitamins and supplements. The person may also want glasses, hearing aids or the names of other clinicians. Ask the office which forms or records are needed rather than collecting more private information than the visit requires.

## How can I include the person during the visit?

Ask whether the person wants you to come into the appointment. They may want a ride and prefer to speak with the doctor alone, or they may welcome you as a note taker. Ask how they want you to help before the visit begins.

Let the person answer the doctor's questions first. Add details only when they want you to, or when the doctor asks for information the person may not recall. Speak to the person, not only to the doctor. If they need time to answer, wait rather than filling every pause.

The person may want private time with the doctor for a personal concern. Respect that request. If you have a concern that is hard to raise in front of the person, ask the clinic how to share a written note discreetly. Be clear about what you observed and avoid asking the clinic to conceal information or make decisions without the person's involvement.

## What questions can help make the plan clear?

Ask for an explanation in plain language if something is unclear. The person and supporter might ask:

- What should we watch for, and when should we contact you?
- What choices are available, and what are the possible benefits and downsides?
- What is the next step, and who will arrange it?
- When should we follow up?

These are examples, not a checklist that must be completed at every visit. The person may have other questions or prefer to focus on one concern. Ask the doctor to recap the main points before you leave.

## What should I write down after the appointment?

Write a short summary of the doctor's explanation, agreed actions and follow-up timing. Note who will handle each action, such as calling the clinic or booking another appointment. If the person agrees, share the details with the family member or helper who needs them.

Keep health notes private and easy for the person to find. Avoid sending them through a shared account or group message if the person has not agreed to that. If an instruction is unclear once you get home, call the clinic and ask for clarification. Do not start, stop or change medication based on your own notes without checking with the prescriber.

Before you leave, check whether a follow-up visit or test needs to be scheduled and who to contact with questions. The person may want to keep a copy of the written plan or choose a trusted helper to hold it. Ask rather than deciding for them.

## What if the person becomes suddenly confused?

Do not wait for a routine appointment if the person suddenly becomes confused. The [NHS advises getting immediate medical help](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/), because sudden confusion can have causes that need urgent attention. Use local emergency services or follow the urgent-care process where you live.

A gradual change can also be worth discussing with a doctor, but it is different from sudden confusion. If you are unsure whether a change is urgent, contact a qualified medical service for guidance rather than trying to diagnose it yourself.

## How can I support the person if appointments feel stressful?

Ask what would make the visit easier. The person may prefer a familiar appointment time, a shorter wait or a trusted companion. Call ahead to ask the clinic about access needs, hearing or language support, and whether questions can be sent before the visit.

If the person is anxious, explain the plan in a calm and direct way that suits them. Bring a familiar item or something to do while waiting, if the person wants it. Ask how much notice they prefer: some people want time to prepare, while others may prefer a short reminder closer to the visit. Use an honest explanation that works for them.

A good visit leaves the person heard, with the next steps understood. Prepare together, protect privacy and ask the clinic to clarify anything that is still uncertain.
