When dementia changes someone a child loves, they may notice before an adult starts the conversation. The child may feel worried, angry, embarrassed, guilty or unsure how to act. Each child and family is different. Clear information and steady support can help a child make sense of what is changing without asking them to carry adult responsibilities.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to talking with children](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/kids/talking-kids-about-dementia) recommends answering questions honestly in words children understand. [Dementia Australia's family guide](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-connected/dementia-and-family-relationships) also stresses honest conversations, reassurance and protecting a young person's space to be a child. Use these ideas as a starting point, not a script for every family.

## How do I begin a conversation about dementia?

Start with a change the child has seen or a question they have asked. You might say, "You have noticed that Grandma repeats questions. She has an illness called dementia that affects her memory and thinking." Use the person's actual diagnosis only if it is known. If the family is still waiting for an assessment, describe the change without labeling it.

Choose a quiet moment when you can stay with the conversation. Use short sentences and pause for the child to respond. You do not have to explain every detail at once. Ask what they already understand and what they want to know next. A younger child may benefit from a picture book or a simple example; an older child may want more detail or time alone to think.

Keep the person with dementia involved when they want to be and can take part. Talk with the child about the person's preferences before sharing private medical details with other people. A child may want a parent or trusted adult nearby when they visit, or may prefer not to discuss the diagnosis with friends or school yet.

## What should I tell a child about dementia?

Explain the parts that matter in the child's daily life. Dementia is an illness that can change how a person remembers, thinks or communicates. It is not contagious, and the child did not cause it. The [Alzheimer's Association](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/kids/talking-kids-about-dementia) notes that children may worry about catching dementia or blame themselves when adults are distressed. Answer those fears directly.

Be honest about what you know and what you do not know. You might explain what the family has noticed, what help the person receives and what plans could affect the child's routine. Avoid promising that the person will get better, that nothing will change or that you know when a change will happen. If you do not know an answer, say so and offer to find reliable information together.

Children may need to hear the same explanation more than once. This is normal. Leave room for the child to ask again later, and check what they understood without turning the conversation into a test. If the child uses different words for the illness, listen first and gently offer clear language they can use.

## How can I answer hard questions?

Respond to the worry underneath the question, then give the clearest honest answer you can. Keep it short and do not fill in details you do not know.

- **"Can I catch it?"** Say no: dementia is not contagious. The child can keep spending time with the person.
- **"Did I make this happen?"** Reassure them that the illness was not caused by something they said or did.
- **"Why does Grandpa call me by another name?"** Explain that dementia can change how he remembers names. It is not the child's fault, and he may not be doing it to upset them.
- **"Will I get dementia too?"** Do not guess about one person's future. Family risk depends on the type and history. If there is a specific concern about an inherited form, ask the person's doctor whether a genetics professional can help.
- **"Will she get better?" or "Will she die?"** Do not promise an outcome or timeline. Tell the child what the care team has said, if the family wants to share it, and explain when you will talk again.
- **"Does he still love me?"** Do not make the child responsible for proving the answer. You can say that the relationship matters and look for a comfortable way to spend time together now.

It is okay to pause if a question feels too large. Tell the child when you will come back to it, and follow through. A calm, truthful "I don't know yet" is more useful than a confident guess.

## How can I help without making the child a caregiver?

Offer connection by choice. If both the child and the person want to, they could listen to familiar music, look at photos, draw, read or share a simple household activity. Our [reminiscence guide](/activities/reminiscence/) and [music guide](/activities/music/) have ideas. Follow the person's and child's responses; it is fine to stop, change the activity or visit another time.

Keep responsibility for care and safety with adults. A child may choose to set out a snack or bring a book, but should not be responsible for supervision, medicines, personal care, appointments or emergencies. Do not imply that visiting, helping or staying close is the child's duty. The [Dementia Australia guide](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/staying-connected/dementia-and-family-relationships) cautions families to respect young people's boundaries and their need to remain children.

Protect time that is just for the child. Keep school, friendships, hobbies and family routines as steady as possible. If caregiving is changing those plans, explain why and look for another adult who can help keep the child's activity or outing. The [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) covers ways to ask other adults to share support.

Before telling a teacher, coach or other adult, include the child in the decision as much as is appropriate. They may want one trusted person to know, or may prefer to wait. If you do share information, agree what to say and what should stay private.

## When should I look for more support?

Ask how the child is doing over time, not only right after the diagnosis. Some children talk openly; others show distress through changes in sleep, school, friendships or behavior. These changes can have many causes and do not prove that a child needs a diagnosis. Listen and ask what support would help.

If the child wants someone outside the family to talk with, consider a trusted teacher, school counselor, pediatrician or child mental-health professional. Ask before contacting school staff about the diagnosis when you can; the child may want to be part of that conversation. Families affected by younger-onset dementia may also need support with work, school and changing family roles.

If you are unsure how to explain a particular change, ask the person's care team or a dementia organization for guidance. The child does not need perfect answers from you. They need honest information, a chance to ask again and adults who keep showing up for them.
