If someone with dementia no longer recognizes you, the moment can feel painful and confusing. You might wonder what to say or whether to explain the relationship again. Each situation is different. Start with what seems to help the person feel safe and comfortable, not with a test of what they remember.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to memory loss and confusion](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/stages-behaviors/memory-loss-confusion) includes not recognizing family among changes that may happen. [Dementia Australia](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/mood-and-behaviour-changes/hallucinations-and-delusions) and [Alzheimer's Society](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/about-dementia/stages-and-symptoms/how-dementia-changes-perception) explain that a person may mistake someone they know for another person. This is general guidance; it cannot tell you why a particular change is happening.

## What might be happening when the person does not recognize me?

Dementia can affect memory and the way a person interprets what they see. Someone might mistake you for another relative or be unsure how you are connected. This does not tell you exactly what they feel about you. Focus on their comfort and response in that moment.

Recognition can also be affected by poor lighting, vision or hearing problems, illness, medicine side effects, an unfamiliar place or a change in routine. These are possible factors, not a way to diagnose the cause. If the change is new or keeps upsetting the person, tell their clinician what happened and ask whether they should be checked.

If a room is dim or busy, try one change: turn on a light, reduce background noise or move somewhere familiar. Ask whether the person wants their glasses or hearing aids if they use them. These steps may help, but they will not explain every instance of non-recognition.

## What can I say in the moment?

Approach where the person can see you and speak in a calm, familiar voice. Say your name and relationship simply, as you might when greeting them on another day: "Hi, I'm Alex, your daughter." Then pause and notice how they respond.

- **If they seem at ease,** continue with an ordinary conversation. Talk about what is happening now, or ask whether they would like to sit together.
- **If they seem unsure,** offer a short reassurance: "You're safe. I'm here with you." Give them time to respond.
- **If they seem afraid or ask you to leave,** step back and give them space. Do not force a hug, insist they recognize you or keep repeating information that seems to upset them.
- **If they call you by another name,** you can introduce yourself once without turning it into a correction. If they remain comfortable, move on to a topic or activity they enjoy.

The [communication guide](/caregiving/communication-tips/) recommends short, calm language and avoiding quizzes or arguments. Try not to ask, "Do you remember who I am?" The question can feel like a test and may not help the person recognize you.

## Could a familiar cue help?

Sometimes a cue may help the person place you, but it will not work for everyone. Try one gentle cue at a time, and stop if it adds pressure.

- **Say your name naturally.** A familiar voice or a brief introduction may be enough.
- **Use a photo or shared object.** If the person enjoys photos, you could look at one together without asking them to name anyone. Our [reminiscence guide](/activities/reminiscence/) has ideas for using photos without quizzing.
- **Try an ordinary shared activity.** A familiar song, drink or task may make the moment easier. Follow the person's response; it is fine to change the subject or finish the visit.
- **Reduce confusion around you.** Check that the room has enough light and that the person has their glasses or hearing aids if they use them. A quieter place may help if many people are talking.

Do not keep adding cues if the person remains uncomfortable. A long explanation or a series of photographs may overwhelm them. Being calmly present can be enough; it is also okay to take a break and try again another time.

## How can I make visits easier?

If the person lives in a care home or sees several helpers, ask the people who know their routine when they tend to feel most comfortable. You might visit at a quieter time, go with someone they recognize or bring one familiar item. These are options to consider, not a schedule that every family must follow.

Before a visit, decide what would feel manageable for you too. You could plan one short activity, bring a photo album or simply sit together. If the person does not seem to know who you are, you can still respond to their needs in the moment. Do not make the visit depend on whether they say your name.

It is understandable to feel sad, rejected or frustrated. Talk with someone you trust, a counselor or a caregiver support group if the experience is weighing on you. The [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) includes ways to ask others for support.

## When should I ask for medical help?

If not recognizing someone is a new pattern, happens often or causes distress, tell the person's doctor. Note when it happens and what else was going on, such as a change in health, sleep, lighting, hearing, medicine or surroundings. Do not stop or change medicine based on a general article.

Sudden confusion or an abrupt change in behavior or health needs urgent medical attention. The [NHS guidance on sudden confusion](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/) says to get medical help immediately. Use the local emergency or urgent-care system where the person lives.

Not being recognized can be hard for both people. Keep your words simple, offer a cue only when it helps and let the person's comfort guide what happens next.
