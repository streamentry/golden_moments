A meal can be harder to follow when the room is noisy, the table is crowded or there are several choices at once. A few small changes to the setting and pace can make it easier for someone with dementia to take part. Start from the person's own habits and preferences, and keep what already works.

The [Alzheimer's Association's food and eating guidance](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/daily-care/food-eating) and [Dementia Australia's mealtime advice](https://www.dementia.org.au/living-dementia/home-life/eating-and-dementia) both recommend a calm setting, familiar foods and enough time. This guide covers the meal environment and everyday support. It is not a nutrition plan or swallowing assessment.

## How can we make a meal feel social without adding pressure?

If the person enjoys company, sit and eat with them. Keep conversation simple and let them focus on the meal when they want to. Avoid correcting how they use a utensil or asking questions that turn the meal into a test. If the table becomes noisy or busy, reduce the number of people or move to a quieter place.

When cooking has become tiring, a familiar prepared meal or help with one part of the task can make the meal easier to manage. Dementia Australia suggests home-delivered or prepared meals as options when needed. Ask which foods the person likes and what kind of help they would accept; convenience should still leave room for preference.

## How can I make the table easier to follow?

Make the meal look simple before changing what is served.

- **Reduce noise.** Turn off the television and lower other competing sounds. Seat the person away from kitchen traffic if that helps them focus.
- **Clear extra items.** Put out only the utensils and seasonings the person will use. Move napkins, flowers or other objects that could distract or be mistaken for food.
- **Use clear contrast.** A plain plate that contrasts with the placemat or table can make the food easier to see. Avoid busy patterns on plates and cloths.
- **Check the light and temperature.** Make sure the person can see the plate, and test food and drinks before serving so they are not too hot.
- **Keep a familiar routine.** If a certain place, time or table arrangement is comfortable, try to keep it consistent. Change only what is causing a problem.

## What should I serve?

Start with foods the person recognizes and enjoys. Offer one course at a time or a choice between two familiar items, rather than a full plate with many separate foods. Smaller portions can be refreshed if the person wants more.

Preferences may change. If a favorite food is refused, avoid arguing or insisting. Offer another familiar option, let the person take a break, or try again later. A meal does not need to follow a rigid schedule if the person is tired or upset; keep regular meal times where they work, and adjust the plan to the person's needs.

If cooking has become difficult, a family member may help with preparation while the person joins in with a safe task they enjoy. Our [household tasks guide](/activities/household-tasks/) has ways to adapt kitchen jobs without turning them into a test.

## How can I support the person's independence?

Let the person eat in the way that is comfortable and safe for them. They may prefer a bowl, a spoon, finger foods or a smaller portion. Do not rush to correct a spill or take over a task the person can still do. Offer help when it is wanted or needed, and allow enough time for the meal.

Eating together can make the meal feel social, if the person enjoys company. Sit nearby, keep conversation easy and let the person focus on eating when they need to. Avoid questions that test memory or comments about how much they have eaten. If the person pauses, wait and ask whether they are ready for more.

## What if mealtimes become stressful?

Pause and look for a practical reason. The room may be noisy; the food may be unfamiliar; the person may need to use the bathroom, rest or change position. Try one adjustment at a time, such as clearing the table, offering water or moving to a quieter place. If the person is distressed, stop and try again later rather than pressing them to finish.

If the person often skips meals, seems uncomfortable, has trouble using their mouth or hands, or has a new change in appetite or weight, write down what you notice and talk with a doctor or dietitian. New trouble chewing or swallowing, repeated coughing with food or drink, or choking needs professional advice. A speech-language pathologist can assess swallowing and advise on safe strategies. Do not change food texture, thicken drinks or try feeding techniques based on a general checklist.

A single unfinished plate does not explain why the person stopped. Look for a pattern across meals: a particular food, time, noise level or seating position may be making the task harder. If a familiar favorite is suddenly difficult, check for pain or dental discomfort and ask a health professional rather than assuming the person is simply being fussy.

For medical questions or a special diet, ask the person's health professional. Do not start a restrictive diet or change medicines to alter appetite without advice. The [brain-health diet guide](/brain-health/diet/) discusses research on diet and dementia risk; it is separate from this practical mealtime guide.

## Should a meal always be eaten at the table?

No. If sitting at the table is tiring or confusing, another comfortable place may work better, as long as the person can eat safely. A smaller meal, snack or pause can be a better fit than trying to finish a full meal on a schedule.

If eating has become difficult over several days, or you are concerned about food, fluids or swallowing, contact a health professional. Seek emergency help for an immediate choking emergency.
