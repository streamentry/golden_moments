An LGBTQ+ person's identity is one part of who they are. Ask what matters to them, use the words they choose, and let them decide who may know personal details. Do not assume a person's identity, partner, family support or privacy wishes from appearance, old records or who visits.

The [Alzheimer's Society's guide to supporting an LGBTQ+ person with dementia](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/get-support/help-dementia-care/supporting-lgbtq-dementia) recommends asking about the person's wishes and avoiding assumptions about relationships. The [Alzheimer's Association's LGBTQ+ dementia resources](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/how-alzheimers-impacts-different-groups/lgbtq-dementia-resources) also describes concerns about stigma and finding welcoming care. People differ; use these ideas as prompts for conversation, not a checklist that defines someone.

## How can I ask about name, pronouns and privacy?

Choose a private, unhurried moment. You might ask, “What name and pronouns would you like us to use?” or “Is there anything about your identity or relationships you want people supporting you to know?” The person can answer partly, say no, or come back to it later. You do not need to ask personal questions that are not relevant to their care.

Ask who may know what. Someone may want their partner acknowledged by staff but not want their wider family told about their sexual orientation or gender identity. A person can also prefer different wording in different settings. Don't share identity details with relatives, visitors or staff simply because you know them.

If the person wants a preference recorded, ask what to write and who should see it. Check how the provider handles a name that differs from other records, including whether staff can use the person's preferred name in conversation. Keep the person's privacy in view; not everyone who helps with care needs every detail.

Preferences can change. Check again when the person can express a choice, especially before a move or a new care arrangement. If it is unclear what they want shared, do not assume. Follow any clear preference they previously gave and ask a qualified local professional for help with questions about care authority or information-sharing rules.

## Who should be included in care conversations?

Ask the person who they trust and want involved. Their support network may include a spouse, partner, close friend, relative or “chosen family”—people who support them as family even if they are not related. Some people rely on family of origin; others do not. Let the person describe their relationships in their own terms.

Ask practical questions: Who may visit? Who should staff call if plans change? Who may receive an update? If a provider requires permission or paperwork before sharing information, ask what it needs and explain the options to the person. Being a partner or chosen supporter does not automatically establish legal authority to make decisions; rules differ by location.

Use the relationship name the person prefers. A care worker who calls a partner a “friend” or “relative” when the person wants them acknowledged as a partner may cause distress. A short, permission-based note can tell staff how to introduce people and what details may be shared.

## What can I ask a care provider?

When comparing residential care, in-home support or adult day services, ask the person which questions they want to raise and whether they want you there. The [SAGE guide to affirming services](https://www.sageusa.org/resource/10-tips-for-finding-lgbt-affirming-services/) notes that sharing personal information with a provider can feel vulnerable. Ask the provider to explain its current practices in plain terms.

- How do you record a preferred name and pronouns? Which staff can see that information?
- How can a person choose who visits and have private time with a partner or other supporter?
- How do you learn and respect the person's preferences around rooming, clothing and personal care?
- What training do staff receive about LGBTQ+ older adults and dementia?
- How can a resident or supporter raise a concern about privacy or disrespect, and what happens next?

The [SAGE and Human Rights Campaign Foundation guide to inclusive long-term care](https://www.sageusa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/lei-consumer-guide-finding-lgbtq-inclusive-ltcc.pdf) suggests checking written policies on visitation, rooming and staff practice. It was published in 2021; use it for questions to ask, not as a statement of current local law. Policies and services differ by place.

A logo, brochure or one reassuring answer cannot guarantee what everyday care will feel like. If the person wants to visit, notice how staff address them and respond to their partner or chosen supporters. Ask to speak privately with a staff member if that would help the person feel safe.

## How can I support the whole person?

Ask about the person's ordinary interests, routines, language, culture, faith, friends and community. Their identity may be important to discuss, or they may prefer to spend time on other parts of life. Do not turn every visit into a conversation about identity or assume that every LGBTQ+ person has had the same experiences.

Some people have faced rejection or discrimination and may feel cautious about services. Others may not see this as a concern. Listen without asking the person to explain or prove anything. If a memory, photo or story about the past is upsetting, ask whether they want to stop or switch topics.

If you are a partner, friend or family member, your own support matters too. Ask the person how they want you involved, and look for a local dementia or LGBTQ+ aging organization if you need advice. The [Alzheimer's Association resource page](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/how-alzheimers-impacts-different-groups/lgbtq-dementia-resources) links to community and caregiver resources; what is available depends on where you live.
