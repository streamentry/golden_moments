Living far away can make it harder to know what help is needed, but distance does not remove every way to take part. You may be able to arrange services, handle a task the person agrees to, or support the person who provides day-to-day care. Start by asking the person with dementia what they want and who they want involved.

The [Alzheimer's Association's long-distance caregiving guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-distance-caregiving) describes roles such as arranging appointments, researching local services, coordinating with nearby helpers and giving an everyday caregiver a break. The [Family Caregiver Alliance also notes](https://www.caregiver.org/faq/how-can-i-help-with-caregiving-from-far-away/) that finding services and planning visits can be difficult from far away. This guide offers general planning ideas; local services and rules vary.

## What can I do from a distance?

Choose practical tasks that fit what the person wants and what you can reliably do. Support can include:

- **Arrange appointments.** Ask whether the person wants help contacting a clinic, keeping a calendar or planning transport.
- **Research local services.** Look for meal delivery, transport, home support, adult day programs or caregiver groups where the person lives. Check details with the provider; availability differs by area.
- **Handle agreed paperwork or orders.** You may help organize forms or arrange supplies if the person asks. Financial, insurance and health records need extra care; use our guide to [money support and scam safety](/caregiving/managing-money-with-dementia/) and check any authority or permission requirements.
- **Make time for the local caregiver.** During a visit, ask what specific task or break would help. You might arrange a meal, cover a planned appointment or spend time with the person while the local helper rests.
- **Stay connected in a way the person enjoys.** Ask whether they prefer a phone call, video call, letter or visit. A call can be a conversation rather than a test about meals, medicines or memory.

The [Alzheimer's Association notes that people who provide remote support may not be able to judge needs directly](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/stages-behaviors/early-stage). When possible, connect with the people who see the person day to day and arrange a visit to the person's own environment. That perspective can add useful information, but it is not a substitute for asking the person or getting a professional assessment.

## How can I coordinate with local helpers?

First ask the person whom they want involved and what updates they are comfortable sharing. They may want one relative to coordinate appointments, a neighbor to check on a specific issue, or no routine check-ins at all. Do not add someone to a care conversation just because they live nearby.

If the person agrees, set up a simple contact plan with the people they choose:

- **Choose one routine contact.** Agree who will send ordinary updates and how often.
- **Name a backup.** Decide who can check in if the usual local helper is unavailable.
- **Separate routine updates from urgent concerns.** Agree how a local person should reach you if the person's safety or health changes suddenly.
- **Keep tasks clear.** Write down who will handle appointments, transport, service calls or household help. Ask before changing the plan.

The [Alzheimer's Association recommends regular communication with family, neighbors and care providers](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-distance-caregiving). You can also ask the local helper what support they need. A short, specific offer may be easier to use than a general "let me know if you need anything."

## What information can a doctor or service provider share with me?

Ask the person's doctor or service provider what permission or paperwork it requires before sharing updates. The [Alzheimer's Association notes that a provider may need a release before discussing care with a remote relative](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-distance-caregiving). Forms and rules vary, so ask the provider rather than assuming that a family relationship gives access.

Respect the person's wishes about who receives information. Share only what is needed for the agreed task, and keep contact details or notes private. If a legal document or formal authority is involved, ask a qualified professional in the person's location what it covers. This guide does not determine anyone's legal authority.

## How can I make a visit useful for both of us?

Ask the person how they would like to spend the time. Leave room for a meal, a walk, music or a quiet conversation, not only appointments and errands.

If the person wants help with coordination, use some of the visit to meet local helpers, speak with a clinician or check whether an arrangement is working. Ask before reviewing personal spaces or records. Share concerns calmly and focus on a specific change, not a judgment about how well someone is coping.

The [Alzheimer's Association suggests using visits to reconnect and to coordinate with local caregivers](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/care-options/long-distance-caregiving). If you notice a concern, discuss it with the person and the people they trust. A single visit cannot establish the full picture of someone's health or ability.

## What if the person's needs or support change?

Review the plan when the person asks for different help, a routine becomes harder or a local helper cannot continue. Ask what the person wants to keep doing and which task needs attention now. A doctor, social worker or local aging service may help identify options. For information about local help, see our guides to [in-home care](/caregiving/choosing-in-home-dementia-care/) and [living alone with dementia](/caregiving/living-alone-with-dementia/).

Make sure there is a local person who can respond to an urgent situation. Our [emergency-planning guide](/caregiving/emergency-planning-dementia/) can help the person and their chosen helpers write down contacts and a backup plan. For disagreements about who should do what, see [family conversations about care roles](/caregiving/family-disagreements-about-dementia-care/).

Distance can limit hands-on help, but it does not decide how much someone cares or what they can contribute. Keep the plan based on the person's wishes, the actual tasks and the support available nearby.
