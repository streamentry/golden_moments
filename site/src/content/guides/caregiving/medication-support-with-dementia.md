Medicine routines can change as needs change. A person may want help remembering a dose, reading a label or refilling a prescription, while still wanting to manage other parts themselves. The goal is to support safe use without taking over more than necessary.

The [Alzheimer's Association medication-safety guide](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/safety/medication-safety) recommends working with doctors and pharmacists, keeping an up-to-date list and agreeing on a routine. This page is general information. Follow the instructions from the prescriber and pharmacist for the person's medicines.

## How can I include the person in the medicine routine?

Ask what help the person wants. They may prefer a reminder, help opening a container or a family member to check the list. If the person can manage some medicines independently, keep that ability in view. A new reminder system should be discussed with them and adjusted if it feels intrusive or confusing.

Use clear, adult language. If the person takes a medicine at a usual time, connect the reminder to a familiar routine only if that matches the instructions from the pharmacist. Give the person time to respond. Do not quiz them, shame them for forgetting or argue about whether they already took a dose.

If a reminder is not working, ask what part is difficult. The label may be hard to read, the container may be hard to open or the medicine may cause an unpleasant feeling. A doctor or pharmacist can help identify options. Do not change the medicine routine by guessing.

## What information should be on a medicine list?

Keep one current list with the name of each prescription medicine, its dose and schedule, and why it was prescribed if known. Include nonprescription medicines, vitamins, herbal products and supplements. Note allergies or past side effects and the names of the clinicians who manage the person's care.

Bring the list to medical appointments and update it when a prescriber changes something. If the person sees more than one doctor, share the list with each care team member only as the person agrees. Keep the list somewhere the person and chosen helpers can find it, and protect it as private health information.

Ask the doctor or pharmacist to check for possible interactions when a new medicine is prescribed. If the person has a new problem after a medication change, write down when it began and contact the prescriber. A change in behavior can have many causes, so do not assume the medicine is responsible without professional advice.

## Can a pill organizer or reminder help?

A calendar, written chart or pill organizer may help some people. The right tool depends on the person's medicines and abilities. Ask the pharmacist whether a pill organizer is appropriate before transferring medicine from its original packaging. Some medicines need special handling or storage.

Agree who will fill or check the organizer and how you will avoid missed or duplicate doses. If more than one person helps, use one shared method for recording what has been given. If the person wants to manage the organizer themselves, ask the pharmacist how to make that safe and workable.

If a dose is missed, check that medicine's written instructions or call the pharmacist before deciding what to do. Do not guess or double a dose based on a general guide.

## What if the person refuses or has trouble swallowing?

Pause and ask what is wrong. The person may dislike the taste, feel sick, be tired or not understand what the medicine is for. Offer a clear explanation and time to respond. Do not force a medicine into the person's mouth or hide it in food without agreement and professional advice.

Never crush a tablet, open a capsule or mix medicine into food unless the prescriber or pharmacist confirms it is safe. Some medicines may not work as intended or can cause harm if changed. If swallowing is difficult, ask whether a liquid or another form is available. Contact the doctor or pharmacist rather than altering the dose yourself.

## How should medicines be stored and handled?

Store medicines according to the label and pharmacist's instructions. If the person might accidentally take an extra dose, ask the person and care team what storage or support is appropriate. A locked cabinet may help in some situations, but access and independence matter too. Review the plan as the person's abilities change.

Keep expired or discontinued medicines separate and ask a pharmacist how to dispose of them safely. Do not keep old medication as a backup or share medicine between people. If a medicine is missing or a dose may have been repeated, contact a pharmacist, poison-control service or emergency service for prompt advice.

## When should I seek medical help?

Contact the doctor or pharmacist if the person has troubling side effects, difficulty swallowing, repeated missed doses or a sudden change after a new medicine. Ask for clear written instructions. Do not stop or reduce a medicine on your own.

If you think the person took too much medicine, call your local poison-control service or emergency services right away. If the person suddenly becomes confused, the [NHS advises immediate medical help](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/confusion/). Use local emergency services or the urgent-care process where you live.

For routine appointments, the [doctor-visit guide](/caregiving/doctor-visit-with-dementia/) can help you prepare questions and a medicine list. The [hospital-visit guide](/caregiving/hospital-visit-with-dementia/) covers sharing medicine information with a care team.

The safest routine is one the person accepts and the care team understands. Ask questions, keep the list current and get professional advice before making a change.
