When a family member has dementia, relatives may disagree about safety, care or the future. One person may see the daily difficulties; another may visit less often or remember the person as they were before. Money, distance, work and family roles can add pressure. Conflict is painful, but it does not by itself show that anyone cares less.

The [Alzheimer's Association guide to family conflicts](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/resolving-family-conflicts) recommends listening without blame, discussing specific caregiving tasks and returning to the conversation as needs change. [Dementia Australia](https://www.dementia.org.au/news/how-do-i-navigate-changes-my-relationships) also notes that families can disagree about care decisions and may need outside support. This guide offers a way to talk; it does not resolve legal authority or decide what care is right for one person.

## How can we keep the person at the center?

Ask the person living with dementia what matters to them and how they want to be involved. A diagnosis does not remove their place in conversations about their daily life. Ask whom they want included and what information they are comfortable sharing. If they want help expressing a preference, give them time and discuss one choice at a time.

The [guide to first steps after a diagnosis](/caregiving/after-dementia-diagnosis/) has ideas for involving the person in future planning. If the person has chosen someone to help with decisions, include that person in the discussion while respecting the person's privacy and wishes. If family members disagree about legal decision-making authority, ask a qualified local professional rather than guessing.

## How should we start a family conversation?

Choose a calm time before a crisis when possible. Decide on one topic for the discussion, such as who can provide rides or how to arrange the next appointment. A long list of difficult decisions can make it harder for everyone to listen.

Before the conversation, write down what you have seen and what you are assuming. For example, “Dad missed two appointments this month” is a specific observation. “Dad can no longer manage anything” is a broad conclusion that may not reflect his abilities or preferences.

Give each person a chance to explain what they are worried about. Listen without interrupting, blaming or comparing how much each person contributes. Someone who lives far away may not see daily needs; someone providing most of the care may be exhausted. Both experiences matter, even when they lead to different views.

## What should we discuss in the meeting?

Keep the conversation practical. A short agenda can help the family leave with a clear next step:

| Topic | Question to discuss |
| --- | --- |
| The person's wishes | What does the person want to keep doing? Who do they want involved? |
| What is happening now | What did someone observe, and when? What needs a clinician's input? |
| Tasks and limits | What help is needed? Who has the time and ability to take each task? |
| Next steps | Who will do what, by when, and when will the family check in again? |

If an issue needs medical advice, write down the question and ask the care team. The [doctor-visit guide](/caregiving/doctor-visit-with-dementia/) can help prepare for that conversation. Keep disagreements about legal documents, benefits or contracts for qualified professionals who know the local rules.

## How can we divide caregiving tasks fairly?

Fair does not always mean equal. The [Alzheimer's Association suggests making a task list that includes the time, money and effort involved](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/resolving-family-conflicts). Then ask what each person prefers and can realistically do.

Some people may help with regular visits or personal care. Others may be better placed to handle appointment scheduling, transport, meal delivery, paperwork or a weekly check-in. A relative who lives far away can still coordinate calls, look up local services or arrange supplies, if the person agrees.

Put agreed tasks in one shared calendar or written list. Include who is responsible, how often and who to contact if plans change. Review the list if someone's health, work schedule or caregiving capacity changes. The [caregiver self-care guide](/caregiving/caregiver-self-care/) covers ways to ask other people to share support.

## What can we do if we still disagree?

Return to what is known, what the person wants and what question remains unresolved. You may not settle everything at one meeting. Agree on one action that is safe and acceptable for now, then set a time to revisit the larger decision.

If relatives disagree about a move, make the first step gathering information rather than holding a yes-or-no vote. Ask what support is missing at home, what the person would prefer and which options the family wants to learn about. A visit to a care home or conversation with an in-home provider can inform the discussion without committing the person to a move. The [residential care guide](/caregiving/choosing-residential-dementia-care/) has questions for comparing options.

If tension continues, ask a trusted third person to help. Depending on the issue, that might be the person's clinician, a social worker, counselor, mediator or faith leader. A support group can also give relatives a place to talk with people facing similar changes. Professional advice is especially important for medical, legal or financial questions.

If family conflict affects the person's safety or care, contact a qualified local service for help. Do not leave the person caught in the middle of an argument. If there is immediate danger, use local emergency services.

Families will not always agree, and one conversation may not settle the issue. Keep listening to the person, make clear commitments and ask for help before one relative has to carry everything alone.
